# Phase 1 — Defining the standard

## What Phase 1 is for

Phase 1 defines the target the rest of the work aims at: **the kind of design judgment and experience a senior, staff, or lead designer must demonstrably have to be credible to a design leader in 2026** — the qualities we then have to make visible about this specific person. It is framed around the person rather than around "a good portfolio," because a portfolio-shaped question tends to produce a portfolio-shaped, generic answer, and generic is exactly what we're trying to escape.

Framed this way, the output is portable. The same standard governs the portfolio, but also the resume, the LinkedIn profile, the interview answers, the way a case gets talked through out loud. We define the caliber once; every asset is measured against it.

## Why "what leaders want in a portfolio" is the wrong question

Earlier reconnaissance into what design leaders actually say — to each other, in unpolished places, not on stage — makes this reframe necessary rather than stylistic. The part that bears on us:

Polish is now free. AI made a clean, well-spaced, edge-case-aware screen cost nothing, so polish no longer proves a person sweated it — which means the artifact alone no longer separates a designer from a model. Leaders feel this and have quietly *demoted the portfolio from evidence to ante*: "we've already looked at it — open Figma, here's a problem, twenty-five minutes." At the same time "craft" has narrowed to mean visual polish, which orphans exactly the strategy-, research-, and systems-caliber work that is hardest to fake and most senior. And the act leaders privately treat as the real core of the job is the *defensible judgment call* — overriding work that even tested well, on grounds you can articulate and stand behind.

The consequence for us is direct. This is almost certainly the mechanism behind the "reads as real work or not" instinct: in 2026, polish reads as free, and free reads as unreal. What survives that collapse — and what a leader is actually screening for — is visible judgment: the human problem seen clearly, the hard decision made and defended, the convergence ("which one, and why") that AI's cheap divergence has made scarce. So the standard cannot be about how the portfolio looks. It has to be about what judgment it makes legible.

## The final outcome of Phase 1

A written standard that names, specifically and testably, the design experiences and qualities of judgment this person must make visible to be read as senior, staff, or lead caliber by a design leader in 2026 — grounded in what those leaders actually reward, not in portfolio convention, and portable across every asset: portfolio, resume, profile, interview.

Done looks like:

- It is specific enough to *fail* real work — to look at an actual case and say "this doesn't show it" — not merely to praise.
- It survives the swap test: it describes design caliber, not "any senior knowledge worker."
- It engages the 2026 reality above rather than pre-AI portfolio virtues — it assumes polish is free and asks what is left.
- Every item is something an asset can be measured against, so Phases 2 and 3 inherit a real rubric, not a mood.

How the standard gets researched and written is Phase 1's own work to determine; this document fixes only what it must arrive at. The reconnaissance aims the target — Phase 1 still does the looking, and may sharpen or overturn what the reconnaissance suggests.
