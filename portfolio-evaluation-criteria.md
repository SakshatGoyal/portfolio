# Portfolio Evaluation Criteria

## Stage one: the portfolio screen

**Eight scored criteria. Scale: 0 = not demonstrated, 1 = weak, 2 = credible, 3 = strong.** "Not demonstrated" means the portfolio does not show it, not that the candidate lacks it.

### 1. Problem altitude and stakes

The class of problem the person was handed, and whether it was real. Staff-level problems are questions about whether or what — tied to long-horizon strategy, crossing team boundaries — and they carry organizational stakes: a sponsor, users with something to lose, consequences for being wrong. Personal and speculative projects can show craft but cannot establish seniority.

**Test:** Strip outcomes and process. Read only the problem. Could it have been assigned to a mid-level designer? And can the evaluator identify who sponsored it and who could have killed it? A defined feature, or a project without real stakeholders, caps this criterion at 1 for a staff claim.

### 2. Craft under inspection

The artifacts alone earn the desire to meet their maker, at both distances: a 30-second look at the body of work, and full-attention inspection of any single screen — hierarchy, typography, spacing, states, edge cases. Work shown only at thumbnail size, blurred, or angled to prevent inspection is scored as if the inspection failed.

**Test:** Two looks. First, artifacts only, 30 seconds: does the evaluator want to meet this person? Second, pick one screen at random and review it as if it were a teammate's work before ship: does it hold? Both must pass for a 3.

### 3. Intentionality and proportion

Evidence of deliberate choice rather than generated gloss, in the work and in its container. Somewhere there is a detail a person visibly chose — type set with a point of view, a state designed because someone cared — and the presentation layer never out-crafts the work it presents. A polished shell around unremarkable work is a negative signal, not a neutral one. Disclosed assistance in making the portfolio is acceptable; concealment that the evaluator can detect is not.

**Test:** Can the evaluator point at one specific detail and infer why a person chose it? And is the best-designed object in the portfolio the work, rather than the website? If every choice could be anyone's default, score 1. If the shell exceeds the work, score 0 regardless of the shell's quality.

### 4. Attribution and scope honesty

The boundary between the candidate's contribution and the team's is explicit, and the claimed scope is plausible for the stated role, level, and dates. These are one quality — do the claims match the person's actual position — tested from inside the case and against its own stated facts. Both failure directions count: claiming the team's work in the first person, and crediting so evenly that the candidate's own contribution disappears. The test run showed this is where multi-owner agency and studio work most often fails: memorable projects in which the candidate's specific decisions cannot be identified.

**Test:** For each flagship case, can the evaluator state which artifacts and decisions were the candidate's own, and does that claimed ownership survive comparison with the credits, role title, team size, and timeline the case itself provides? If the contribution cannot be isolated, score 1 at most, regardless of the project's quality.

### 5. Consequence with mechanism, at the claimed altitude

Impact stated as a causal chain — this decision led to this behavior change led to this outcome, with how it was isolated and what it did not fix — and scaled to match the level claimed. A staff case shows consequence at the level of a product line, an organization's velocity, or a strategy. Unexplained percentages are assumed fabricated and score worse than no metrics. Awards and press are evidence of reception, not consequence.

**Test:** For each impact claim: is it falsifiable, does it name its mechanism, and does its scale match the problem's scale? An unexplained number scores 0 for its case. Modest outcomes with honest measurement boundaries can score 3.

### 6. Checkable trail

The portfolio points outward at independently verifiable things: named, findable shipped surfaces; a public record that corroborates the claims; work a findable former colleague would recognize as the candidate's. Full anonymization is a failure — the walkthrough stage exists precisely so confidentiality never requires it.

**Test:** For each major claim, is there a findable surface, person, or record that would confirm it specifically? Does the public trail agree with the portfolio on title, dates, and team?

### 7. Transmission

Whether the argument actually reaches a reader who gives it the real attention budget: partial, fast, and unforgiving. Three components, all required for a 3:

**(a) The problem is humanized.** A reader outside the domain can restate the problem, say why it was hard, and recognize the human stake — without the difficulty being flattened. Naming domain systems is not the same as conveying the problem; "the answer cites Salesforce" communicates nothing, while "the reader cannot tell which field produced the answer, when it was last updated, or whether the reasoning can be inspected" is a problem anyone can feel.

**(b) The writing is economical.** Meaning survives partial reading. Headers, captions, and first sentences alone deliver a true, specific version of the argument. Sentences are short without oversimplifying; challenges, tensions, and consequences are surfaced without filler. The presence of an explanation is never treated as proof the explanation was received.

**(c) The agency is retellable.** After one unaided pass, the evaluator can state in two sentences what the problem was and what this person specifically drove. If the reader can retell the product but not the candidate's contribution, this component fails even when the other two pass.

**Test:** One pass at natural speed, then three questions: restate the problem, quote what the headers alone told you, and say what this person drove. Score the weakest of the three components.

### 8. Staff leverage

Evidence that the candidate's existence made other people better or faster, and that they moved decisions above their own level. The two former criteria are collapsed because they measure the same quality — leverage beyond one's own output — and moved in lockstep in testing. The portfolio can only ever show the *existence* of this evidence: a framework or system other teams adopted after the candidate stopped touching it, a named decision at a higher level that went differently because of them. The *mechanism* — who adopted it, what resistance arose, how the executive was moved — is verified at the walkthrough and interview stages.

**Test:** Does the portfolio name at least one artifact with a life independent of its author, or one specific higher-level decision the candidate changed? If every story ends at "I shipped my project well," the candidate reads as senior; score 0 against a staff claim. For senior-level evaluation, treat this criterion as bonus signal, not a requirement.

## Stage two: the walkthrough

**Three criteria, checked as present or probed — not scored 0–3 at the screen.** These are the qualities the test run showed flooring at the portfolio stage: real, decisive, and almost never visible on a public site, because the research locates them in the private case deck and the live telling. At the screen, the evaluator records only a flag: *visible*, *absent*, or *contradicted*. At the walkthrough, they become pass/fail gates.

### 9. Judgment under tension

Contested decisions — moments where a competent peer might have chosen differently, with the rejected alternative and the reasoning stated — and at least one named failure: a direction that died, a bet that missed, attributed to the candidate's own judgment, with what it changed. These collapse into one criterion because a named failure is the strongest species of contested-decision evidence, and both were absent from all three test portfolios for the same reason: they live in the fuller telling, not the public summary.

**Walkthrough gate:** Across the presented cases, at least three decisions where the evaluator could genuinely disagree with the reasoning, and at least one consequential miss owned without deflection. Narrated process — research, personas, ideation, outcome — does not count and is a negative signal.

### 10. Provenance texture

The unfakeable specifics of presence: the constraint that deformed the design, the stakeholder by role who blocked a version, what got cut in scoping, what shipped differently than drawn and why. This is the primary authorship evidence in an era when polished narrative can be generated. Confidentiality excuses vague evidence — relative numbers, redrawn flows — but never vague texture, which costs nothing to share and everything to invent.

**Walkthrough gate:** Take any substantive stretch of the telling and ask: could a competent designer who was never in the building have produced this? If yes throughout, the case proves nothing about authorship, and the evaluation should not proceed to offer regardless of other scores.

### 11. Leverage mechanism

Where the portfolio asserted staff leverage, the walkthrough establishes the mechanism: who adopted the framework and what they did with it after the candidate moved on; which executive decision changed, what evidence moved it, what resistance arose. Assertion that survives the portfolio but dissolves under this questioning retroactively zeroes criterion 8.

## Scoring discipline

**Stage one produces a profile, not a total.** Eight scores, read against the gates: criteria 2 and 3 gate whether the rest is read at all; criteria 4, 5, and 6 gate trust; criteria 1, 7, and 8 establish level. Do not sum across gates — the test run demonstrated that a raw total can rank candidates in the wrong order, because saturated craft scores average against floored trust scores. A candidate advances when no gate fails, not when the total is highest.

**Stage-two flags are recorded at the screen but never scored there.** A portfolio that happens to show judgment under tension or provenance texture earns a *visible* flag and should be read as unusually strong evidence — precisely because most portfolios structurally cannot show it. Its absence at the screen is neutral. Its absence at the walkthrough is disqualifying.

**Criterion 8 is scored only against the level claimed.** For staff and lead claims it is a hard requirement; for senior claims it is recorded but not required.

**Confidence is part of the record.** Where material was inaccessible or partially retrievable, low scores mean "not demonstrated by accessible material," never "disproved," and the evaluation notes its own reduced confidence — as the test run correctly did.
