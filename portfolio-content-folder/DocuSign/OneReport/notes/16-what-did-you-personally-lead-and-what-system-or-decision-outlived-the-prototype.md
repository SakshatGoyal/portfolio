# 16. What did you personally lead, and what system or decision outlived the prototype?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Attribution, force multiplication, corroboration

**Question:** You state that you set the brief, requirements, interaction model, and mobile designs. What did the Director, PMs, data engineers, analysts, security partners, and other designers contribute; what was uniquely yours; and what framework, semantic model, decision rubric, or design pattern was adopted by other teams after OneReport stopped? Who could corroborate that influence?

**Reason for investigation:** The claimed ownership is unusually broad, but the story contains almost no team boundary, organizational friction, handoff artifact, or evidence that others used the work. The prior dashboard migration provides context for why the designer was trusted, yet this case does not show how the exploration multiplied the effectiveness of other designers or teams. Presenting to the C-suite is access; it is not automatically Staff-level influence.

The force-multiplication gate matters even more because this product never reached users. A public-product leader would look for a reusable strategy or a decision that redirected investment. An enterprise leader might value a canonical mobile analytics framework, metric ontology, security pattern, or feasibility rubric that shaped subsequent internal products.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would draw a precise “I/we” boundary, name the contested cross-functional decision the designer changed, show the artifact others reused, and identify leaders who would confirm the contribution. If the work ended with the designer's prototypes and presentation, I would understand it as a strong individual exploration, not Staff-level force multiplication.

## Answer

The I/we boundary, drawn as precisely as I can. Mine: the brief itself — converting "what would this look like on mobile" into the moments framing and three scenarios; the product requirements; the unit matrix (the metric/dimension/filter audit across the migrated dashboards); the interaction model on all three surfaces — the lens/Focus/Filter semantics, the card-and-sheet content model, the AMA grammar and the search-over-chat decision; every screen and illustration in the artifacts; the testing protocol and its readouts; and the scope fence that fed the sizing memo. Piyush Bhargav, Director of Data & Analytics: the originating ask, data-domain expertise, access to the five directors, and the sponsorship that carried the concept into a C-suite room — he was the reason the work had an audience, and his review sharpened the lens vocabulary in the Analysis surface. The director of product management: the mobile framing of the original prompt, requirements pressure throughout, and the guided-drill-down counterargument (Q5) that forced me to defend the Sankey properly. The analytics engineer: dashboard inventory legwork on the unit matrix and the grain warnings that shaped the Customers lens. The two ex-Cisco engineers with their engineering lead: the entire functional Google Actions POC and the sizing memo — I supplied the grammar and scope; the implementation judgment and the cost credibility were theirs, and the "low-cost AI" claim this project is remembered for stands on their prior Cisco experience as much as my interaction decision. No researcher, no security partner, no other designer touched it. That breadth of solo design ownership is real, and it cuts both ways: it's why the attribution is clean and why the work has the blind spots a team would have caught.

Its afterlife was modest. The unit matrix had afterlife: the analytics team kept the spreadsheet in circulation as a reference during subsequent dashboard consolidation work, because "which role does this unit play on whose dashboard" turned out to be a generally useful question independent of any mobile app. That's a working artifact other people used after I stopped touching it — but it's a spreadsheet with a good idea in it, not an adopted framework, and it does not support a larger claim. The sizing memo's two-column structure — fenced-grammar cost versus open-ended AI cost — was the kind of argument that gets reused in conversations more than cited in documents; I believe it shaped how that engineering group talked about scoping AI asks afterward, and I can't point to an artifact proving it. The design patterns — Sankey lens traversal, the card/sheet model — outlived it only in me; no DocuSign team adopted them, because nothing shipped and no adjacent team was building executive mobile analytics.

Corroboration: Piyush would confirm the ownership boundary and the C-suite presentation specifically; the engineering lead would confirm the grammar-to-POC handoff and that the design fencing made the estimate possible; the director of product management would confirm the contested architecture arguments. Those three could each verify a different load-bearing claim, which is how I'd want it checked.

The verdict on force multiplication: this was a strong individual exploration with one modestly reused artifact and a real influence on one cost conversation — not an organizational capability. The multiplication story in my career sits in the migration before it and work after it; OneReport's contribution to that story is what it taught me about authoring endings (Q14), and I'd rather claim that accurately than claim adoption that didn't happen.
