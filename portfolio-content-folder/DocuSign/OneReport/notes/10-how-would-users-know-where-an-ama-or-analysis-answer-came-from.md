# 10. How would users know where an AMA or Analysis answer came from?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Provenance, craft, consequence

**Question:** What provenance, source, definition, owner, freshness, and confidence information would accompany a number returned by AMA or a path shown in Analysis? Could an executive open the exact source record, see when it refreshed, reconcile conflicts, and understand whether the value was actual, forecast, modeled, or delayed?

**Reason for investigation:** The designs show “updated” timestamps at a report level, but the core retrieval and flow experiences do not visibly explain source lineage or conflicting refresh schedules. This absence matters more because the product serves senior leaders and combines sensitive data from multiple departments. The faster the interface produces an answer, the easier it is to mistake convenience for authority.

At Staff level, trust should be part of the interaction model, not a backend detail left for later. A public-product PM would consider wrong-answer cost and support burden. An enterprise product leader would expect governed definitions, drill-through, audit logs, and a way to resolve disagreement between source systems.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want the proposed provenance model, how it affected visual hierarchy, and evidence that directors cared about or tested it. A strong answer may acknowledge that the concept had not reached this layer and explain what would have to be true before launch. Claiming the experience was ready for feasibility estimation without a trust model would make me question the designer's enterprise judgment.

## Answer

Mostly, they wouldn't — and I'd rather state the gap precisely than gesture at a trust layer that didn't exist. The provenance surface that actually existed in the prototypes was report-scoped: every report carried "Updated: 24 Feb 2023, 11:59 PDT" directly under its title, plus the fiscal-position chip. That placement was deliberate — freshness as headline metadata, not tooltip trivia — and it was also the entire provenance model. The two surfaces where trust matters most had less. An AMA answer returned a large confident number with no source line: nothing saying which system produced 2,125,126, when it refreshed, or whether it was actuals. Analysis nodes carried values and deltas with no lineage at all.

This wasn't unexamined; it was flagged, in testing, by the person best positioned to flag it. The director closest to finance looked at the closed-deals card and asked, almost immediately: "which system is that from?" — because he knew firsthand that sales-ops and finance numbers for closed business disagreed at quarter boundaries, and he knew an executive would quote whichever number this screen showed. That exchange is the single most important thing director testing produced, because it identified the product's sharpest failure mode: the interface's confidence outrunning the data's authority. A polished card makes a number look adjudicated even when it's one system's opinion.

What I did about it was proposal-level, and I'll mark it clearly as such: after that session I sketched a provenance line for the AMA card — source system, refresh timestamp, and an actual/forecast marker sitting directly under the headline number, with the sheet carrying the governed definition on drill-in. It existed as an iteration in my working file; it never made the presented deck, because the C-suite presentation was converging and the provenance answer implied backend commitments (a definitions registry, refresh metadata plumbing) that nobody could stand behind at concept stage. The alternative was to present the provenance layer as designed anyway — it would have demoed beautifully — and I chose not to, on the principle that a trust surface you can't back is worse than a visible gap. In hindsight, I should have shown it as an explicit requirement.

More fundamentally, provenance wasn't a missing label; it was a missing system. The unit matrix work (Q5) was a step toward canonical definitions, but conflicts between sources, refresh-schedule mismatches across lenses, actual-versus-modeled marking, and drill-through to source records were all unbuilt and mostly undesigned. The attainment bars ("104% of forecasted $15.5M") encoded forecast-versus-actual in one narrow place; nothing generalized it. My stated position in the feasibility conversation was that this was a launch precondition, not a polish item: for this audience, a wrong number delivered confidently during a call is the product failing at its core scenario, and no amount of interaction craft compensates. So the scorecard reads: freshness surfaced well at report level; lineage, definition, and confidence absent; the gap identified by testing, acknowledged to the sponsor, and left as the first unfunded requirement of a next phase that never came.
