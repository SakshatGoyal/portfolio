# 13. What did cost and feasibility evaluation conclude?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Consequence, provenance, narrative command

**Question:** The concept was presented to the C-suite and used to evaluate cost and feasibility. What estimates, dependencies, operating costs, risks, and alternatives were actually produced, and what decision did leadership make as a result? Did your design lower projected cost, change sequencing, narrow scope, or reveal that the product should not proceed?

**Reason for investigation:** “Moved toward cost estimation” is the closest thing the story has to an organizational outcome, but the result is absent. The search-over-chat decision is credited with having the greatest impact because it made a lower-cost implementation plausible. Without the estimate or decision, I cannot tell whether that was a meaningful business contribution, a promising conversation, or a prototype that still failed the investment test.

At Staff level, an unshipped exploration can be excellent if it prevents waste or clarifies a strategic option. A product leader accustomed to public products would value a rigorous stop recommendation as much as a launch. An enterprise leader would expect total cost of ownership, data integration, mobile security, maintenance, and support—not only model-inference cost.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want the cost drivers, the designer's contribution to the model, the decision threshold, and the concrete leadership decision. Evidence that the designer changed the investment shape or advised against a poor bet would strengthen Staff-level product judgment. If no decision followed, the case should frame the output as unresolved exploration rather than organizational impact.

## Answer

I'll report what was actually produced, who produced it, and where it ended — including the anticlimax, because the anticlimax is the finding. The evaluation was not a formal TCO study. It consisted of three artifacts: the engineering lead's sizing memo, my scope-definition inputs to it, and the C-suite presentation where concept and cost posture were shown together.

The sizing memo — a few pages, written by the engineering lead with the two ex-Cisco engineers after the POC — priced the AMA path in two columns. Column one: the fenced-grammar approach, intent matching over a governed metric dictionary via Google Actions-class tooling, extended POC to hardened service. Because the POC existed and the grammar was explicit, this column was concrete: a small team, a few months to a pilot-grade service, modest run costs. Column two: the "real AI" approach as understood in that pre-LLM-commodity moment — custom NLU, training data, ML engineering DocuSign would have had to acquire — which wasn't priced so much as declared out of category. That contrast was the memo's argument, and my search-over-chat decision was structurally load-bearing in it: fencing the interaction to short phrases over a bounded dictionary is what made column one a bounded engineering estimate instead of a research project. That's the specific sense in which the case says the design decision enabled movement toward cost estimation. My other contribution was the requirements fence itself — a one-page scope note saying pilot needs the metric dictionary, report content model, and Sankey over pre-aggregated lens tables; explicitly not needed: notifications, offline, per-role experiences, the provenance registry (deferred, flagged as launch-blocking later).

What the memo did not cover is most of the true cost, and the feasibility conversation surfaced exactly that. The costs that dominated discussion were not inference: the semantic layer engineered for real (the unit matrix as running infrastructure, not a spreadsheet); mobile security posture — MDM, review, standing on-call — amortized over an audience of perhaps thirty to fifty people; and permanent data-engineering ownership of executive-grade freshness. Piyush's team could absorb none of that as a side project.

The conclusion: there was no decision meeting, no recorded go/no-go. The C-suite presentation landed well — genuine interest, the low-cost AI posture specifically appreciated, which in that budget climate was the concept's best card. What followed was neither funding nor a kill: the pilot ask was never brought to a prioritization forum with a sponsor willing to trade something for it, and the concept aged out as priorities moved. If I translate the polite version into an investment verdict: the design lowered the projected cost of the novel part (conversational retrieval) far enough to make the conversation real, and the conversation then revealed that the expensive part was never the AI — it was governance, security, and data operations for a tiny population. No one decided to stop it; no one paid for it. At Staff level I have to own the sharper reading: a concept that gains traction but never forces an explicit decision has failed the investment test by default, and the failure mode was mine to prevent — I built a strong artifact and a soft ask. The memo and the fence outlived the project as the reusable parts.
