# 7. Why was “include every metric” treated as a success criterion?

**Category:** The work is understandable but concerning  
**Caliber markers:** Contested decisions, craft, product judgment

**Question:** The Reports section says the card-and-sheet approach allowed you to include every metric without overwhelming viewers. Why was complete metric inclusion desirable for executives, and what evidence showed that progressive disclosure had actually reduced overload rather than merely moved it behind taps? Which metrics were intentionally excluded, summarized, or delegated to a specialist?

**Reason for investigation:** “Every metric” is a red flag in an executive product. The source dashboards were already described as cluttered; preserving all their content may reproduce the original information architecture at smaller scale. The visible report screens contain many figures, percentages, categories, and deep sheets. The news-story metaphor is promising, but the portfolio does not show editorial prioritization, role-based relevance, exception thresholds, or a clear rule for what deserved executive attention.

This matters at Staff level because strategic design is often the ability to remove information and define the decision hierarchy, not just fit more into a polished container. A public-product PM would ask which behavior the content ranking was meant to drive. An enterprise leader would ask who governed executive-level definitions and who bore responsibility when a summarized metric hid an important exception.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would correct or qualify the “every metric” claim, describe a content model and prioritization rubric, and show how role, urgency, confidence, and exception status determined visibility. If completeness was a sponsor requirement, I want to hear how the designer challenged it. An answer centered on sheets as a space-saving device would weaken my confidence in the designer's information-design judgment.

## Answer

Two answers exist, and the case study only tells the flattering one. The easy, real, political answer: every team that produces metrics wants its metrics visible, and this concept lived on director goodwill. I had just migrated fourteen-odd dashboards belonging to five functions; a mobile concept that visibly dropped a function's numbers would have lost that function's director, and director enthusiasm was the only currency the project had. Completeness was partly coalition maintenance. I do not treat that as a larger result.

The substantive answer is a contradiction I've watched play out in every executive review I've supported: leaders say they want a handful of KPIs, and they mean it — until they look at one. The moment a number surprises them, they generate questions that can only be answered by more metrics. GNMRR is down — is that Direct or Digital? Which region? Against forecast or against plan? A five-KPI product is calm until the first anomaly, then it's useless precisely when it matters. So the design target was never "show few things"; it was "make an arbitrarily deep metric space traversable in seconds." Completeness of the space, ruthless hierarchy of the surface.

The mechanics, since this is where the craft lived. Fixed editorial slots: a report leads with its key metric, primaries sit beside it at equal weight, secondary and tertiary material lives below or behind. Cards are headlines — the Business Metrics report shows GNMRR Bookings at $16.2M with QoQ/YoY deltas and two attainment bars (104% of forecast, 78% of plan) and nothing else; the full quarter-by-quarter Direct/Digital table exists only in the sheet you pull up by tapping. And a rule I applied without exception: every dense dataset gets a visual before it gets a table — the GNMRR sheet opens with stacked quarterly bars; the Marketing Insights funnel table carries inline magnitude bars in the count column and a computed funnel-reduction column so the shape is readable before any number is. Progressive disclosure, visual hierarchy, and clean composition did the work.

The acknowledged cost: interaction. Sheets bury things. An anomaly two taps deep is invisible on the card, and "include every metric without overwhelming viewers" can be restated less generously as "moved the overload behind taps" — I can't disprove that restatement, because we never measured comprehension or taps-to-answer. Testing gave weak positive signal — directors found the GNMRR breakdown unprompted, and nobody described a report screen as cluttered, which for this data density genuinely surprised me — but that's five friendly people on placeholder data.

The alternative I raised and lost: an exceptions-first model, where the report leads with the three things that moved abnormally and everything nominal collapses. I sketched it verbally with Piyush; it died on ownership — exception thresholds need an accountable owner per metric, and no such governance existed to hang thresholds on. I judged it unwinnable at concept stage and spent the argument elsewhere. Nothing was formally excluded, summarized-with-authority, or delegated to a specialist — the more accurate reading is that we deferred editorial judgment to structure. Structure was the thing I could actually govern from the design seat.
