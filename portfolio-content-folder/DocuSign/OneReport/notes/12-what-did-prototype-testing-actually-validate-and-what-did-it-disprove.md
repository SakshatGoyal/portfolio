# 12. What did prototype testing actually validate, and what did it disprove?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Decisions, consequence, defensibility

**Question:** How many directors tested which prototype tasks, with what data fidelity, and what were the observed task-success, time-to-number, comprehension, confidence, and error results? What did users fail to understand, and which design did you materially change or discard because of the test?

**Reason for investigation:** The portfolio says the search-bar behavior was “validated through internal testing with directors,” the prototypes were evaluated with other directors, and the framework scaled as more priorities were shared. Those statements are too general to evaluate. There is no participant count, role mix, protocol, before/after comparison, failure evidence, or example of research costing the designer a preferred design.

For an unshipped project, discovery evidence is the outcome. It therefore needs more rigor, not less. A public-product PM would need evidence strong enough to make the next investment decision. An enterprise leader would want to know whether participants behaved differently with realistic sensitive data and whether seniority introduced politeness or sponsor bias.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would present specific tasks, observations, counterevidence, and the decision each finding changed. It would also distinguish desirability testing from usability, data feasibility, and security approval. If “validated” means directors reacted positively during a presentation, I would treat it as stakeholder enthusiasm rather than product evidence.

## Answer

Let me give the protocol at its real size, because "validated through internal testing" deserves numbers even when they're small. Five directors, recruited through Piyush — spanning sales operations, marketing analytics, customer success, finance-adjacent revenue ops, and product — in individual sessions of thirty to forty-five minutes across roughly three weeks. Materials: clickable Figma prototypes of Reports and Analysis on a phone in their hands, plus the live Google Actions POC for AMA. All fabricated data. Tasks, not demos, at least for the first half of each session: find GNMRR attainment against plan; get "closed deals versus pipeline" out of AMA; trace where churn concentrates by product in the Sankey; then unstructured reaction time, which with directors is unavoidable — they narrate what their executive would think, which is proxy testimony, and I logged it as opinion, not observation.

What the tallies actually showed. All five completed the AMA retrieval task quickly and without instruction; several immediately tried short phrases without being told — the search field's affordance doing exactly the behavioral work it was chosen for. That is the legitimate core of the "validated with directors" claim: terse-input behavior emerged unprompted, consistently. Reports navigation held: all five reached the GNMRR sheet from the card unaided, and nobody called the report screens overwhelming, which at that density I genuinely feared. The Sankey split the room: two read it essentially instantly, three needed one guided pass before the flow grammar clicked — and, most usefully, three of five initially misread Focus's greyed flows as removed rather than de-emphasized (Q6). Failures were where the learning was.

Two designs changed because of testing, and one died. The focus dimming was rebuilt — grey flows kept plainly visible rather than faded to near-nothing — directly from the misread. Second, my early Analysis layout had filter chips along the top, web-style; in-hand testing showed directors' thumbs never went there and chips truncated badly over the canvas, which produced the bottom control bar (filter, columns, focus, undo, redo) in the final screens. The death: an early AMA iteration showed a scrolling result history, chat-residue I'd kept without noticing. Watching sessions made it obvious the mid-call scenario has no history mindset — you want this number now — and it was cut for the single-answer card. Research costing me things I'd already drawn is the part of this I trust most.

The limits are substantial. This was concept validation with proxies: five friendly senior people, placeholder data, sponsor gravity in every room — directors knew Piyush championed this, and seniority plus politeness inflates positive signal. No executives, ever. No task-time measurement, no comprehension scoring, no before/after design comparison, no statistical anything — tallies in a notebook, not findings in a report. Nothing tested data feasibility, security posture, or whether anyone would open this app on a real Tuesday. What testing legitimately established: the interaction grammar was learnable in minutes, the search-not-chat behavioral bet worked, the Sankey needed onboarding, and the trust question ("which system is that from?" — Q10) was going to be the product's hardest problem. What it never established: that anyone needed this. I kept those two categories separate in every readout, including to the C-suite, and I'd keep them separate here.
