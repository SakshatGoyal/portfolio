# 3. Who exactly was the user, and what differences mattered enough to shape the product?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Problem altitude, provenance, narrative command

**Question:** How did you segment the intended VP and C-level audience by role, decision authority, data literacy, business unit, geography, and frequency of use? Which capabilities were common enough to justify one product, and which requirements could not be generalized across executives?

**Reason for investigation:** The story repeatedly refers to “VP and C-level leaders” as if they were one coherent user group. The screens then show marketing, sales, renewals, success, bookings, pipeline, revenue, churn, and product flows in the same product concept. That breadth is strategically interesting, but it also makes me skeptical that one interaction model and one navigation hierarchy could serve every role without becoming a lowest-common-denominator executive portal.

This matters because Staff designers operating across a company must distinguish platform-level invariants from local requirements. A public-product leader would expect a clear target segment and prioritization rationale. An enterprise systems leader would ask whether access, definitions, alert thresholds, and acceptable latency differed by executive function.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would describe a segmentation model, identify the primary and secondary users, explain which differences affected the design, and show a deliberate scope boundary. If the designer can explain why the common layer was real and how role-specific content was governed, the cross-functional scope becomes Staff evidence. If every executive was treated as a generic consumer of “business metrics,” I would question both the research depth and the product architecture.

## Answer

We never built a real segmentation model. "VP and C-level leaders" stayed a single nominal audience throughout. What we had instead — and what did shape the product — was a segmentation of the data supply side rather than the user side. During the dashboard audit I mapped which function owned which dashboards and how each function's artifacts referred to the same business units. That map is where the product's real structure came from: the things every function shared (Region, Product, Channel, Partners, Customers, the fiscal calendar) became the invariant layer — the lenses in Analysis and the spine of the semantic model — and the things that were function-specific became content, not structure: individual reports.

You can see the vestige of user segmentation in the Reports surface. The home screen has a "For You" section with DocuSign Business Metrics pinned, then a grid of function-scoped executive summaries — Sales Exec. Summary, Marketing Exec. Summary, Success, Renewal, Partner, Booking — plus a "Recently Added Reports" shelf with artifacts like the Q4FY23 Bundle Performance Summary. The implicit model was: the shell and interaction grammar are universal; role difference is handled by which reports appear in "For You." Subscription, not architecture. A CMO's app and a CRO's app would differ in content ordering, nothing else.

I considered role-based home experiences — a Finance mode with attainment front and center, a Product mode leading with adoption and usage. The director of product management liked that direction; it demos well. I rejected it for concept stage on cost grounds: every role mode multiplies the design surface, and with no actual executives in the loop we'd be inventing differences we couldn't verify. Worse, role modes harden guesses into architecture. A subscription model let us defer the question without hardening an untested guess into the architecture. Without visible role tailoring, however, the product can read as a generic portal. That criticism lands.

The org friction here was subtle: every director who saw the prototype wanted their function's report promoted, which is how the report grid grew as wide as it did. The "framework scaled easily" line in the case study describes this — adding a director's priorities meant adding reports and lens values, not redesigning — but scaling easily is also how you get a lowest-common-denominator portal, and I felt that tension without resolving it.

We never established whether a CFO and a CMO differ in acceptable data latency, confidence thresholds, or alert appetite — the enterprise-leader questions — because we never spoke to either. The one difference we did act on was data literacy direction: everything was designed to be readable without analyst training (attainment bars, plain-language deltas), on the theory — stated in our sessions, untested — that an executive's data fluency is high on judgment and low on tool patience. If the project had continued, user segmentation was the first thing the next phase needed; I said so in the feasibility conversation, and I'd say it here again.
