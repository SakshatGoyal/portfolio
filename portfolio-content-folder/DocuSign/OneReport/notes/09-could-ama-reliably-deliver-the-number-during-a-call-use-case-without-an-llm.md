# 9. Could AMA reliably deliver the “number during a call” use case without an LLM?

**Category:** The work is understandable but technically unconvincing  
**Caliber markers:** Decisions, provenance, defensibility

**Question:** What query types could the search-based AMA experience actually resolve, how were natural-language phrases mapped to governed metrics and time ranges, and what happened when a phrase was ambiguous, unsupported, or matched multiple definitions? What did the low-cost Google Action-style implementation prove beyond the visual impression of AI?

**Reason for investigation:** Choosing a search field instead of a chat box is a thoughtful behavioral cue: it encourages terse input and avoids implying a long conversation. The case then makes a much larger claim—that sophisticated-seeming AI interaction could be achieved with low-cost frameworks and that this decision unlocked cost estimation. The artifacts show progressive phrases such as “closed deals,” “closed deals vs pipeline,” and “over time,” but do not show entity resolution, synonym handling, context carryover, clarification, error recovery, or the data-query architecture.

For a public-product PM, the question is whether the search interaction improved time-to-value and repeat success. For an enterprise leader, a wrong number uttered confidently during a call is a serious trust failure. At Staff level, the designer must distinguish interaction simulation from a feasible product system.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would name the supported grammar, show prototype failures and clarification patterns, explain how metrics were resolved to canonical definitions, and separate what was functional from what was mocked. If the prototype only demonstrated that a search box felt cheaper than chat, I would value the interface instinct but discount the technical and organizational impact claim.

## Answer

Within a deliberately fenced grammar, yes — and the fence was the design. AMA's resolvable query shape was narrow and explicit: a metric phrase, optionally a comparator, optionally a time or breakdown modifier. The three-panel artifact in the case is literally the grammar demo: "Closed deals" resolves to the closed-deals card — 2,125,126 this quarter, QoQ and YoY deltas, a Count/$-Amount toggle. Append "vs pipeline" and you get the paired bar comparison, 2,450,125 against 2,125,126. Append "over time" and it becomes a five-quarter line chart. Each utterance is the previous one plus a suffix; that's context carryover as string composition, which is exactly as unsophisticated as it sounds and exactly as cheap.

The mechanism was an entity dictionary, not language understanding. On the order of thirty canonical metrics, each with synonym lists ("closed deals," "closed won," "deals closed" → the same governed metric), a small set of comparators, and time-range tokens defaulting to current fiscal quarter — the default the fiscal countdown chip made legible everywhere else. The consequential ambiguity decision was this: where the metric had two readings — closed deals as a count versus a dollar amount — we resolved to one and surfaced the fork as a control (the Count/$ toggle on the card) rather than asking a clarifying question. In a call, a clarifying question is a failure; a defensible default plus a one-tap correction is not. The search bar itself carried the behavioral contract: a search field says "short phrases suffice," where a chat box implies conversation, patience, and a system that can hold one. Both decisions came from the same premise — never promise more understanding than the backend has.

The POC is where I got structurally lucky. Several of our engineers had come from Cisco, where they'd built internal Google Actions integrations with good results. When I framed AMA as intent-plus-slot resolution over a fixed metric dictionary, the engineering lead essentially said: we've built this before; give us the grammar. Two of them stood up a functional Google Actions POC — intents for metric phrases, slots for time and comparator, answering from a static extract of migrated dashboard data, not the live warehouse. My low-cost instinct and their prior expertise meeting was a happy accident, and it mattered: the POC is what let the concept move toward real cost estimation at a moment when AI projects were presumptively too expensive. It never went into development — that boundary is absolute — but it pushed the needle from "sophisticated AI, unaffordable" to "intent matching, priceable."

What broke, concretely, in director sessions: causal questions — "why is churn up" — fell entirely outside the grammar; the system could show churn, never explain it. Unmodeled entities failed — "closed deals in EMEA" died until region slots were added to the dictionary, which took the engineers under a day, and that cheap extendability was itself a finding. Bare homonyms ("bookings" colliding with GNMRR bookings) resolved by dictionary priority, sometimes wrongly. And there was no error recovery design — no "did you mean," no graceful no-match state; misses just failed. I never designed those states, and I'd flag that as the biggest gap between the concept and a product: the demo proved the happy grammar was cheap; it proved nothing about being wrong gracefully, and during a live call, wrong-gracefully is most of the product.
