# 1. What evidence established that an executive mobile product should exist at all?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Problem altitude, consequence, defensibility

**Question:** Before choosing a mobile application, what evidence showed that VP and C-level leaders had an important, recurring decision problem that existing desktop dashboards, scheduled reports, alerts, email, or an executive analyst could not solve well enough? What decision was being delayed or degraded, how often did it occur, and what would the company gain if OneReport solved it?

**Reason for investigation:** The story begins with the Director of Data & Analytics asking whether the migrated dashboard data could be made available to executives on mobile. That is a sponsor request, not yet a product problem. The portfolio says little was known about how executives used company metrics, but it moves quickly from that uncertainty to three scenarios and a three-surface app. I do not see evidence of the severity, frequency, or strategic cost of the underlying problem, nor a comparison with cheaper ways to serve a very small population.

This matters at Staff level because the designer claims ownership of the brief and requirements. A Staff designer should be able to turn an executive request into a “should this exist?” decision, not merely give polished form to the request. A public-product PM would want an investment thesis and opportunity-cost argument. An enterprise leader would also want to know whether a bespoke executive application created support, security, and data-governance costs disproportionate to its value.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would name the decisions at stake, the current workaround, the executive population, the recurring moment of failure, the cost of delay or error, and the evidence that eliminated lower-cost options. It would improve my view of the designer's product judgment even though nothing shipped. If the answer is essentially “a director asked for mobile,” I would read the work as a feature exploration led by an influential sponsor, not Staff-level product framing.

## Answer

There was no validated problem. The project began as a sponsor prompt. I had spent roughly six months — the contract started in August — leading the migration of internal dashboards for product, finance, marketing, sales, and the executive team. Early the following year, Piyush Bhargav, the Director of Data & Analytics, asked for a conversation and put the question directly: could we make all of this data available to C-level and VP-level folks? In the same kickoff conversation, the director of product management sharpened it to "what would this look like on mobile?" That's the entire origin. No decision log of delayed executive decisions, no diary study, no severity estimate.

What I can claim is that I refused to treat the prompt as a requirements document. The premise I could actually defend was mechanical, not aspirational: executives move constantly, they work through conversations, they are almost never at a desk, and the phone is the one device always on them — everything else is mediated by their staff. During the migration I had also seen something suggestive, which I treat as an informal observation and not a metric: when the analytics engineer and I went through access lists to plan cutover, VP-level accounts were conspicuously thin among active users of the dashboards nominally built for them. The data existed; the intended senior audience wasn't touching it in that form. That's not proof a mobile app should exist. It's proof the desktop form factor wasn't being consumed by this population, which made "same data, different moment" at least a testable framing.

The concrete decision was how to treat the ask. One alternative was to insist on a discovery phase first: interview executives, study calendars, then decide. I chose instead to treat it as a concept investment with deliberately cheap kill criteria: low-fidelity prototypes, director-level evaluation, and a feasibility estimate as the exit gate. My reasoning was constraint-driven. We had no access channel to executives for research — the data sensitivity that later constrained testing also constrained discovery, and asking Piyush to broker C-suite interviews for a project that didn't exist yet was politically implausible. A discovery-first plan would have died in scheduling. A concept you can put in front of directors in six weeks at least generates a real conversation about whether the thing should exist.

The friction that deformed the work was exactly that access problem. Every "user input" we got came through directors acting as proxies for their executives, which means the evidence base was secondhand by construction.

I cannot name a specific delayed decision, a frequency, or a cost of error, because we never established them. If you read this as a feature exploration elevated by an influential sponsor, you're reading it correctly — that is what it structurally was. The Staff-level contribution was the conversion: turning "can we put this on mobile" into a falsifiable premise about moments of phone use, a three-surface model that could be evaluated cheaply, and an exit gate (cost and feasibility) that treated "don't build this" as an acceptable answer. The project's value was always going to be the quality of that decision, not the app.
