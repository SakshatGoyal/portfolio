# 5. Did the Sankey interaction solve a semantic problem, or only visualize it?

**Category:** The work is understandable but unconvincing  
**Caliber markers:** Craft, contested decisions, defensibility

**Question:** The story says a business unit could appear as a metric, dimension, or filter depending on the dashboard, and that the solution was a Sankey-style analytical flow. What was the underlying semantic or data-model problem, how was it normalized, and why was Sankey the right interaction rather than merely a visual layer over inconsistent definitions?

**Reason for investigation:** This is one of the most ambitious claims in the case. A shared analytical layer across Product, Channel, Partner, Region, and Customer could be a genuine Staff-level systems contribution. But the visible solution is primarily a navigation and visualization pattern. A Sankey diagram can show relationships; it does not by itself reconcile conflicting metric definitions, grain, hierarchies, double counting, fiscal calendars, or source ownership.

The mobile screens also show dense crossing flows, numerous labels, repeated quarter-over-quarter values, and small touch targets. I can understand the concept, but I am not yet convinced that an executive could accurately answer a high-stakes question faster than with a well-structured table or guided drill-down. A public-product PM would ask for task success and comprehension. An enterprise leader would ask who owned the canonical metric ontology beneath the screen.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would separate the semantic-layer work from the visualization, name the data contracts and definition conflicts resolved, and show comparative testing against other analytical models. It would also explain when Sankey was intentionally not used. If the designer only rearranged dashboard fields into flows, I would credit exploratory craft but not the claimed platform-level analytical architecture.

## Answer

Both, and the actual split matters. Let me start with the underlying problem as I actually encountered it. Auditing the migrated dashboards — around fourteen substantive ones across five functions — I built a spreadsheet the analytics engineer and I called the unit matrix: business units down the rows, and for each function's dashboards, whether that unit appeared as a metric, a dimension, or a filter. "Channel" was the canonical offender: marketing dashboards had Direct and Digital as metrics (spend per channel, headline numbers), finance treated Channel as a dimension you broke revenue down by, and sales dashboards used it as a filter checkbox. Same word, three grammatical roles, three implicit denominators. Every function's dashboard was internally consistent and mutually untranslatable.

The normalization move was to stop assigning units a fixed role. In the model I designed, every business unit belongs to a lens — Channel, Partners, Region, Product, Customers — and a lens can play any role depending on where you put it: as a column in the flow, it's a dimension; as the quantity being traced, it feeds the metric; in Focus or Filters, it's a selection. That's why the case says Focus and Filters "pick from the same dataset and subsets as the business lenses" — that sameness is the whole idea. One vocabulary, three grammatical positions.

Why Sankey specifically, rather than a drill-down table or treemap? Conservation. A Sankey makes the denominator physically visible: flows entering a column must sum to flows leaving it. On the Churn Flow screen, Total Churn 300k splits into Direct 200k and Digital 100k, and those recombine across Partners, Region, Product, down to named customers. If a breakdown double-counted or dropped records, the picture would visibly leak. That property is exactly the discipline the source dashboards lacked, and it's why I'd argue the interaction embodies the semantic contract rather than just decorating it. Sankey was also deliberately not universal: trends got their own tab, because time series aren't flows, and forcing them into one would have been visualization vanity.

The model normalized interaction semantics, not the data. The real reconciliation work — definition ownership, grain, fiscal alignment, the ontology under the screen — was never engineered. The analytics engineer flagged the sharpest instance: the Customers lens changes grain from aggregates to entity-level rows, which is why the mock caps it at six named customers plus an "Others: 162k" bucket — a design dodge for an unsolved cardinality problem. And the screens themselves confess their fidelity: every QoQ delta reads ±12.87%, a placeholder repeated verbatim across every node. Nobody should mistake those screens for a working analytical layer, and I never presented them as one.

The contested decision: the director of product management argued for a guided drill-down — tap a metric, pick a breakdown, get a bar chart — as more learnable. He was probably right about learnability; three of the five directors we tested with needed the first Sankey explained. I held the line because drill-down re-fragments the semantics: each path becomes its own little dashboard again. The tradeoff I accepted was a steeper first read for a consistent underlying grammar.

Verdict, in my own words: a genuine semantic model expressed and enforced at the interaction layer, sitting on top of a data layer that remained hypothetical. Solved in design, sketched in contract, unbuilt in fact.
