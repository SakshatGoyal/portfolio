# 11. How was sensitive executive data protected on a mobile device?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Provenance texture, decisions, defensibility

**Question:** What security and privacy constraints visibly shaped the product—role-based access, device management, authentication, session timeout, screenshot prevention, notification redaction, local caching, offline behavior, lost-device response, cross-region restrictions, and access revocation? Which desirable interaction was changed or rejected because of these constraints?

**Reason for investigation:** The Apple Note uses data sensitivity to explain why testing could not be large-scale, but the case study does not show that the same sensitivity materially deformed the design. A mobile executive app containing revenue, pipeline, churn, customer, and product data has a high consequence of exposure. Yet the visuals largely present an ordinary consumer-style mobile shell with no visible security states or permission boundaries.

The Caliber Standard expects at least one real constraint that changed the design. For a public-product PM, security is part of product viability. For an enterprise leader, it may be the determining factor in whether the product can exist at all.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would identify the security partner, threat model, specific design consequences, and unresolved launch blockers. It could also explain why a native app was preferable or inferior to a managed web surface. If sensitivity appears only as an excuse for thin research and not as a product constraint, I would doubt the provenance and readiness of the work.

## Answer

Sensitivity shaped the process profoundly and the visible design only modestly, and that asymmetry is the headline. Process first: sensitivity is why testing stayed small and director-level — this data could not circulate for large-scale research — and it's why every prototype ran on fabricated values. Look closely at the screens and you can see the fabrication: QoQ deltas repeating ±12.87% everywhere, a marketing report whose "Total Spent" reads $275.12. Real numbers never entered a Figma file or the Google Actions POC's static extract; that was a hard line the analytics engineer and I held from day one, and it cost us realism in testing — directors reviewing placeholder data behave differently than they would with their own live numbers, and I can't quantify that distortion.

Design consequences that genuinely traced to sensitivity: two. First, the killed notifications scenario (Q2) — the decision that revenue figures would never appear on a lock screen or travel through notification channels predated any screen design, and it removed an entire product surface. Second, session posture: the working assumption, discussed with Piyush, was corporate-managed devices, SSO, short session timeouts, and no offline caching of report data — assumptions, stated in conversations and reflected only negatively in the design (no offline mode was ever drawn, deliberately). What never happened: no security partner was engaged, no threat model written, no screenshot-prevention, redaction, lost-device, or cross-region access design produced. The visuals present a consumer-clean shell because the security layer was assumed, not designed.

The contested decision inside this: native app versus managed web surface. The engineering lead raised it directly in the feasibility conversation — a web surface behind existing SSO inherits the company's security perimeter almost for free, while a native app with local state means MDM policy, app-level encryption review, and a standing mobile security obligation for an audience of maybe forty people. I had designed native-first for interaction reasons (the Sankey's direct manipulation, sheet physics, the mid-meeting speed budget), and I'll admit the choice was made on experience quality before security economics. We left it unresolved, although managed web was the more defensible choice on shippability.

The friction point material: sensitivity operated on this project mostly as a veto held by everyone and specified by no one. There was no security stakeholder in the room to negotiate with — which felt like freedom and was actually risk, because it meant every security question was deferred rather than answered, accumulating silently at the exit gate. In the feasibility conversation, security burden for a tiny population surfaced as one of the load-bearing reasons the product economics didn't close (Q14), and none of us could rebut it with design evidence, because the design had never engaged it.

Sensitivity materially deformed the research method and killed one scenario, but the concept never earned the claim of being a secure product design. If I reran this, a security architect joins in week two, and the redaction, timeout, and revocation states get designed as first-class craft — partly because they're the states most likely to determine whether the product can exist at all, and partly because designing them is how a concept proves it understands its own deployment reality.
