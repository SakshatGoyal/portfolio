# 12. How was a “similar customer” cohort made statistically and commercially defensible?

**Category:** The work is understandable but materially concerning  
**Caliber markers:** Enterprise craft, consequence, defensibility

**Question:** How did the system select peer cohorts from Salesforce attributes such as region, segment, industry, sub-industry, department, and use case; what minimum sample, recency, outlier, and confidence rules applied; how did it handle sparse combinations; and what prevented an AE from fine-tuning a cohort until it supported the desired sales narrative?

**Reason for investigation:** The comparison-criteria design thoughtfully defaults to context-specific attributes and allows an AE to override them because “a comparison the AE cannot defend in front of the customer is useless.” The same flexibility creates a cherry-picking risk. The artifacts do not show cohort size, confidence intervals, range stability, missing-data warnings, representativeness, or a record of how the cohort changed. The supplemental metric treats fine-tuning as healthy within a range, but fine-tuning itself does not establish truth.

A public-product PM would worry about misleading customer claims and long-term trust. An enterprise systems leader would also examine privacy thresholds, row-level access, data quality, and whether peer groups exposed sensitive commercial information indirectly.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would explain the cohort algorithm, governance, minimum-N rule, confidence display, audit trail, and user constraints. It would include a case where the system refused a comparison. If the design relied primarily on AE judgment without statistical and policy guardrails, I would consider the customer-facing use unsafe regardless of desirability.

## Answer

The cohort logic was the most contested piece of engineering-adjacent design in the whole project, so let me give it in full. Default construction: exact match on the selected account's own Salesforce attributes — region, segment, industry, sub-industry, department, use-case business process. Context Specific mode reads those from the account record; Custom mode lets the AE override any of them. The two fields that made the idea viable at all — use-case and sub-industry — were the overlooked ones I'd found already populated in the Alation schemas. The design principle, printed on the artifact: the default must be defensible in front of the customer, and the AE owns the final cohort because they own that conversation.

Sparse combinations were the fight. One of the data analysts ran distribution counts for me on the six-attribute exact match: the median cohort landed in the low teens, but the tail was ugly — plenty of sub-industry × use-case × department combinations produced cohorts under five, some of one or two, which is both statistically meaningless and a genuine privacy hazard, because a cohort of two in a named sub-industry is trivially identifiable by a knowledgeable AE. That forced the minimum-N debate. The analyst argued for twelve on stability grounds — under twelve, the extents whipsaw with every joining or leaving account. The senior data engineer argued five was workable if we showed ranges rather than individual traces. I designed to eight, with a degradation ladder rather than a cliff: below minimum, the comparison panel refuses — a "not enough similar accounts" state — and offers to widen, dropping attributes in a fixed order: department first (least commercially meaningful for peer identity), then use-case, never sub-industry or segment, because those two are what make a cohort recognizable as "my peers" to a customer. The refusal state was a deliberate feature: a tool that will decline to draw a chart is a tool whose charts mean something.

Anti-cherry-picking rested on one structural choice: the comparison criteria render adjacent to the chart, as part of the same visual unit. Any snapshot an AE captures carries its cohort definition on its face. You can fine-tune a cohort until it flatters — the design cannot stop a motivated narrator — but you cannot present the flattering cohort while concealing what it is; the customer sees "Region NA, Segment Enterprise, Industry Business Services, Sub-Industry Services and Logistics" printed beside the medians. Self-documentation was the guardrail I could actually build.

Several important controls did not survive to ship or were never built. Confidence display — I mocked interval bands and a cohort-size readout ("n=14 similar accounts") — was cut in the shipped build; the chart shows median and extents without stating cohort size, which I consider the single worst delta between design and ship on this product. The minimum-N refusal existed as a designed state; enforcement in the shipped version was softer than my spec, and I couldn't verify the hard floor was wired before my involvement ended. There was no audit trail of cohort edits — no record of how a cohort was tuned before a snapshot — so the fine-tune metric in the later registry (15% floor, 45% ideal) was a designed target with no pipe to feed it. Recency and outlier rules stayed simple: trailing-window data from the standard extracts, extents as percentile-based ranges to blunt outliers, and nothing more sophisticated. Row-level security rode on the existing Tableau/Salesforce permission model rather than anything cohort-specific. If an enterprise data-governance reviewer had audited FUB at ship, they'd have passed the intent and the criteria transparency, and failed the confidence display and audit trail. They'd have been right on both counts.
