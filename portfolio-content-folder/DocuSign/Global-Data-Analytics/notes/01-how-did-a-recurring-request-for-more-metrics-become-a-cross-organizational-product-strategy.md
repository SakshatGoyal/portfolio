# 1. How did a recurring request for “more metrics” become a cross-organizational product strategy?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Problem altitude, force multiplication, provenance

**Question:** You describe a broad responsibility to design dashboards for many organizations and a culture in which PMs repeatedly asked to add metrics. How did you turn your frustration with that pattern into an approved strategic question about decision support, who sponsored it, which teams' roadmaps changed, and what 12-month product direction followed?

**Reason for investigation:** This is potentially the Staff-level center of the story. The designer appears to have noticed a systemic failure—dashboards accumulated data but did not make judgment easier—and initiated research rather than accepting the next feature request. The source material says a director approved a research plan and that the work spanned Sales, Marketing, Finance, Product, and Operations. But the organizational mechanism is vague: I do not know what mandate the director granted, whether PM priorities were displaced, which leadership decision changed, or whether these two tools remained exceptions inside the same metric-accumulation culture.

A public-product PM will want to see how an insight changed strategy, prioritization, or investment. An enterprise leader will want to know whether the designer created a repeatable decision-support approach across internal products. At Staff level, “I independently did valuable research” is promising but insufficient unless it changed how other teams worked.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would name the sponsor by role, the resistance, the resource or roadmap decision won, and the artifact that let multiple PMs distinguish reporting requests from decision-support opportunities. If the designer's intervention became a framework used beyond these dashboards, it would be force-multiplication evidence. If it was approved as a one-off experiment without affecting product strategy, I would read the work as strong senior initiative rather than Staff-level organizational influence.

## Answer

This was a capital transaction, not an epiphany. For my first six months at DocuSign — I came in through the Intelligaia contract in August, owning dashboard design across Sales, Marketing, Finance, Product, and Operations — I did nothing strategic at all. I led the migration of every metric, not just the dashboards, into Tableau. It was unglamorous work through real ambiguity: metric definitions that contradicted each other across orgs, owners who had left, a reporting suite that had to be redesigned wholesale. I delivered fast and I made the ambiguity someone else no longer had to hold. That was the entire basis of my influence. Nobody sponsors a contractor's research plan because the plan is good; they sponsor it because the person asking has never cost them anything.

The mechanism of the ask mattered as much as the capital. I took a research plan — well thought out, deliberately not polished, because a polished deck would have looked like a product pitch — to the Director of Product Management who sat over several data verticals. I priced the yes as low as I possibly could: this is pure understanding-how-people-work research, there is no product commitment, no roadmap ask, no headcount. If I had asked for anything more, I believe he would have said no, and I knew it. I considered writing the full opportunity brief and asking for a discovery sprint on the roadmap. I chose the cheap yes because a contractor's first strategic ask sets the ceiling for the second one, and I wanted a second one.

What did the mandate actually buy? Access, mostly. It let me recruit AEs through the sales-ops manager without a manager asking why a designer was on their calendar, and it gave me a sentence — "the director approved this" — that ended most objections. It did not displace anyone's priorities. No PM roadmap changed on day one. The displacement came later and it was traded, not granted: when the research produced the two hypotheses, I got build capacity from the platform PM by absorbing dashboard-request triage for his verticals for a quarter, and the director quietly deferred a Marketing dashboard refresh to make room. That deferral was a real cost paid by a real team, and the marketing-analytics PM did not thank me for it.

On the 12-month question, I have to be careful not to overclaim. The research did produce a direction — dashboards as decision instruments rather than metric displays — and the two tools that followed carried it. What I cannot claim is that it changed the culture. The "add more metrics" requests kept arriving through the entire engagement; I built a small triage artifact — a one-page set of questions distinguishing a reporting request from a decision-support opportunity — and two of the four data PMs used it in intake conversations. The other two ignored it, politely. The organization's operating model did not convert. Two tools got built inside a metric-accumulation culture that outlived them, and if I present this as "I changed how DocuSign thinks about analytics," a skeptical interviewer should catch it. What I actually did was buy, with six months of delivery, one narrow window in which a design contractor could set the question instead of receiving it — and then spend that window almost entirely on sales, for reasons I get into under Q2 that were as political as they were analytical.
