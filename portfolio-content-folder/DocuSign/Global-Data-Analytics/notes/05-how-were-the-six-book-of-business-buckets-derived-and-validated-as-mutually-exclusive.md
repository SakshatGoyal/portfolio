# 5. How were the six Book of Business buckets derived and validated as mutually exclusive?

**Category:** The work is understandable but not yet convincing  
**Caliber markers:** Contested decisions, defensibility, craft

**Question:** What evidence and analytical method produced Strategic, Growth, Analysis, Efficiency, Deferred, and Alternative Engagement segments; why did every account need to fit exactly one bucket; what edge cases broke the model; and how did you validate that the buckets predicted a meaningfully different action rather than merely describing accounts?

**Reason for investigation:** The six-bucket model is the product's intellectual core, yet its derivation is summarized as synthesis through iterations. The visible cards have plausible definitions, but several depend on combinations of MRR, usage, renewal risk, and AE judgment that may overlap. “Alternative Engagement” also appears partly as a quota-management fallback rather than a customer state. Mutual exclusivity can simplify a UI while hiding a world in which one account is simultaneously high-risk, high-value, and growth-ready.

At Staff level, the designer should be able to defend the model three whys deep and show what it deliberately sacrificed. A public-product PM would ask whether a segment changed prioritization or outcomes. An enterprise leader would ask how rule changes were governed and whether different coverage teams required different models.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would show the raw patterns, alternative models considered, classification rules, validation examples, misclassified accounts, and the action attached to each bucket. It would also explain why a mutually exclusive model was preferable to multi-label risk and opportunity signals. If the buckets mainly made the dashboard orderly, I would not accept them as Staff-level product reasoning.

## Answer

The derivation was a synthesis of six AEs' personal heuristics, and I can walk the mechanics. From the generative interviews and the first focus session, six participants gave me explicit bucketing logic. Two were MRR-and-usage people: high-MRR/low-usage means worry, low-MRR/high-usage means growth. One was product-centered — she isolated customers by which products they ran and watched power users. One watched support-ticket trajectories as his leading indicator. Two were blends. I put each heuristic on the whiteboard — that's the synthesis photo with "Using MRR & Adoption as a framework" and "Individual users over customer account" clusters on it — and ran a thought exercise: if this AE had to reach five specific customers this quarter, what would they check, in what order? The interaction points, compiled across all six, collapsed into a small set of controls; the recurring destination states became the buckets.

Four of the six buckets are data-describable: Strategic (high MRR, high adoption — the safe upsell bet), Growth (low MRR, high usage), Analysis (high MRR, low usage — the churn-risk quadrant), Efficiency (low MRR, low usage — renewal filler that neither grows nor churns). The other two are not data states at all, and this distinction matters: Deferred is an AE's explicit choice to de-prioritize, and Alternative Engagement is a quarter-relative fallback — the backup list when an AE feels they may miss target. You cannot compute those from telemetry; they are declared judgments. So the model is four quadrants plus two judgment states, and the marketing-friendly claim that "every customer fits exactly one bucket" is really a precedence artifact: declared states override computed ones, and the quadrant split is exhaustive by construction once you fix an MRR threshold and a usage cut.

Edge cases broke it in predictable places. A high-MRR, high-usage account with a support-ticket spike is Strategic by quadrant and a fire by reality — the model has no "at-risk Strategic" state, which is why the Support Tickets trend chart sits on the main surface rather than inside any bucket. Mid-term usage crashes flip an account between quadrants across refreshes, so bucket membership is unstable near the threshold — an account at $1,010 MRR and one at $990 are different colors on the chart while being the same customer in every meaningful way. Parent-child accounts with multiple subscription owners were the worst: "one account, one bucket" only holds at the level you aggregate to, which is why the Trend Lines control lets you pivot between Accounts and Subscription Owner.

The alternative was a multi-label model — independent risk and opportunity flags per account — and I sketched it. I rejected it for two reasons: the AEs I studied genuinely worked from mutually exclusive piles ("who am I calling this week" is a partition, not a tag cloud), and exclusive buckets sold to product owners as a simple filter story where multi-label read as a data-science project. What I sacrificed is real: the model describes accounts more than it prescribes actions, and I never ran the validation that would have earned the stronger claim — take a quarter of bucket assignments, track which accounts the AE actually touched, and test whether bucket membership predicted different treatment. The buckets made prioritization legible and fast. Whether they made it better is a claim I designed the instrumentation to test and never got to run.
