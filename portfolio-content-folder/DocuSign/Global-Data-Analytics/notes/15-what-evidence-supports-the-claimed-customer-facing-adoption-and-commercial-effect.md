# 15. What evidence supports the claimed customer-facing adoption and commercial effect?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Consequence, provenance, defensibility

**Question:** How many AEs exported or captured snapshots, how often and for how many accounts, what evidence shows the snapshots appeared in customer decks or QBRs, and what supports the statement that customers were more willing to buy features or upgrade after seeing peer evidence? Was any opportunity-level funnel or holdback analysis performed?

**Reason for investigation:** The case says users included snapshots in decks and that customers became more willing to pay. The supplemental report appropriately downgrades this to reported behavior and a qualitative commercial signal because there is no instrumented adoption rate, control group, conversion rate, opportunity linkage, or incremental revenue. The designed targets—25% snapshot adoption, 60% ideal, repeated account use, and cohort fine-tuning—are not achieved outcomes.

This distinction is central to Staff credibility. A public-product PM will separate usage, influence, and revenue. An enterprise leader will also ask whether snapshots were accurate, current, permission-safe, and linked to the source view.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would provide observed counts, interview quotes with context, opportunity linkage if available, and explicit limits on commercial attribution. It would propose a credible phased or matched comparison without presenting it as completed. If the designer repeats the willingness-to-pay claim as fact, I would question the reliability of all stated metrics.

## Answer

The evidence is thin because a crucial part of the instrumentation never shipped. My instrumentation proposal for Feature Usage Baseline specified events: baseline views, cohort edits, and — critically — snapshot exports, with an in-tool export affordance so that "snapshot taken" would be a logged act tied to an account. What shipped had no export affordance; AEs captured snapshots by screenshotting the view. A screenshot is invisible to telemetry. So the single instrument that would have made snapshot adoption countable never existed in production, and every downstream number the measurement registry defines — snapshot adoption 25/60, snapshot depth 40/70 — was designed against capability that didn't ship. The registry labels them as targets; this is the mechanical reason they could never have been anything else.

What I actually had: Tableau server logs — view counts and unique-viewer counts, which I checked informally and which showed steady repeat viewership among the rollout population, distinguishing the tool from shelf-ware but saying nothing about external use. Direct observation: three AEs, in follow-up conversations, showed or described decks with baseline snapshots in them — I saw two decks myself; one was QBR material for a logistics-vertical account, with the comparison-criteria block visible in the pasted image, which pleased me more than it should have given everything else missing. Relayed reports: the sales-ops manager and one PM independently described additional AEs doing the same. Frequency, per-AE account coverage, persistence across quarters: unknown, uninstrumented.

On willingness to pay: the claim's provenance is AE statements — customers responded more openly to feature proposals when shown peer evidence, some moved toward upgrades in conversations where snapshots were used. Nobody performed opportunity-level linkage. No snapshot was ever associated with an opportunity record; no funnel event, conversion comparison, or holdback existed. The published sentence that customers "were more willing to pay" is a faithful report of what account teams said and an unfaithful impression of what was measured, and in any live setting I decompose it exactly that way: reported behavior, qualitative signal, zero commercial attribution. The strongest form I'll state: the tool demonstrably entered real customer-facing artifacts for some AEs, and the users doing so attributed conversational value to it. That's adoption-of-workflow evidence, not revenue evidence.

The credible path to more — which I proposed while there, and which the later registry formalizes — was modest: an export affordance to make snapshots loggable, a Salesforce association ("attach to opportunity") at export time, and a phased rollout across teams to give a comparison structure without pretending to run an experiment on sellers. None was built before my engagement ended; the accurate classification is proposed, not performed. The obvious question is why I did not hold ship for the export affordance. The answer is the real trade: the build was in Tableau, where a governed export-with-logging affordance was genuinely awkward, the sponsor wanted the tool in AEs' hands inside the quarter, and I judged shipping-then-instrumenting better than delaying a validated workflow for measurement plumbing. The consequence of losing that argument with myself is this answer: a tool people evidently used in the wild, and a case study that can only say "evidently" instead of a number.
