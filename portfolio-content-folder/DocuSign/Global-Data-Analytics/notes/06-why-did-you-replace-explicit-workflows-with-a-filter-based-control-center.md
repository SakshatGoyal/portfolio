# 6. Why did you replace explicit workflows with a filter-based control center?

**Category:** The work is understandable but under-explained  
**Caliber markers:** Contested decisions, craft, defensibility

**Question:** You began by designing workflows to identify the six customer types and later simplified them into filters and trend controls. What failed in the workflow approach, what did filters enable, what guidance or automation was lost, and how did you verify that users could still translate patterns into action?

**Reason for investigation:** This is a genuine contested decision, but the case compresses it into one sentence. Filters can respect expert flexibility and reduce prescriptive friction. They can also push the cognitive work back onto the user—the exact problem an “action-oriented” product was meant to solve. The final dashboard presents several trend charts, term-start constraints, plan and ownership controls, an MRR threshold, and high/low toggles. It is not obvious that a user managing hundreds of accounts knows which combination to apply or what to do next.

A public-product PM would ask whether the change improved task success, not simply interface simplicity. An enterprise leader would ask whether filters preserved expert discretion while supporting consistency across AEs.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want the competing prototypes, the observed failure, and the measures used to compare them. A strong answer would show that the workflow over-constrained diverse books and that the control center produced faster, better prioritization with adequate interpretation. If the simplification was driven mainly by build constraints, that can still be good judgment—but the constraint and accepted downside should be named.

## Answer

The workflow version existed as sketched flows, never interactive, and it died on branching math before it died on politics. The concept was a guided sequence for finding each customer type: pick your objective (find churn risk, find growth candidates, build a renewal list), set your term window, set your threshold, review the candidate list, save it. Five steps, reasonable on paper. When I walked the sketches against the six AEs' actual heuristics, the branches multiplied: the support-ticket AE needed a different entry step than the MRR-quadrant AEs; the product-centered AE needed feature selection before thresholding; book size changed whether you could review candidates as a list or needed another narrowing pass. I counted eleven distinct branch points to honor the observed diversity. A workflow that branches eleven ways isn't a workflow; it's a decision tree wearing a wizard's hat, and every branch was another surface to design, build in Tableau, and maintain.

The filter panel was the collapse of that tree into simultaneous controls. Every workflow step became a control that's always visible: Term Start, Plan, Charge Model, Features Used, Subscription Owner, the MRR threshold, the High/Low/All view toggle, the trend-line pivot. Any AE's personal path through the old tree is now just an ordering of adjustments, and the interface never reconfigures — "designing around fewer clicks, without the interface moving or changing a lot" was the actual pitch line, and it worked on product owners precisely because a static filter panel is cheap to build and impossible to get lost in. That sellability was a legitimate engineering-cost argument, and it was not purely user-centered: given a contractor's shipping window and Tableau as the delivery medium, the filter panel was buildable and the workflow engine was not.

What was lost is exactly what the question suspects: guidance. A filter panel presumes the user knows which combination expresses their intent. My mitigation was interpretive scaffolding rather than automation — the insight-grid pattern where chart families carry the sentence they answer: "the conservative customer finding more value than they anticipated," "the over-buyer who may partially churn next quarter," "a sudden rise of issues with a high-stakes customer," "an anomaly to investigate before my next call." Those captions are the ghost of the workflow: they teach the reading, not the clicking. The segmentation-model cards served the same purpose on the definitional side.

Verification was informal and small. I ran think-aloud sessions with four AEs from the research pool against the near-final design, giving tasks like "find the accounts you'd worry about this quarter." All four got to defensible account sets; two of the four narrated the interpretive captions back to me unprompted, which told me the scaffolding carried. What I did not do was compare workflow-vs-filter on task success, because the workflow version was never interactive — the comparison the question asks for was structurally impossible with the sketches I had. The defensible claim is: the filter model was verified adequate with a small sample; the workflow model was rejected on branching complexity and build cost, not on head-to-head evidence. A hybrid — one guided path for the modal AE and filters for the rest — was on my whiteboard, but I cut it for scope. On a longer runway, it's the version I'd have tested.
