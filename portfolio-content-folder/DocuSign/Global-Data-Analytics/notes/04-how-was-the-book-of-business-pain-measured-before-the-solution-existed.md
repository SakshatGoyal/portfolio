# 4. How was the Book of Business pain measured before the solution existed?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Consequence, provenance, defensibility

**Question:** Before designing Book of Business, how did you establish the frequency and cost of the twice-weekly AE–BDR workflow across spreadsheets and internal documents? What steps, handoffs, data reconciliations, errors, and downstream decisions did you observe, and how did the baseline differ by book size and role?

**Reason for investigation:** The problem statement is strong and concrete: AEs and BDRs jointly updated documents twice a week to organize data, prioritize accounts, and spot anomalies. But the supporting evidence is largely narrative. The later 4–6-hour saving claim makes the absence of a clear pre-product baseline more consequential. I cannot tell whether the tool eliminated work, shifted it to another role, reduced a periodic peak, or simply made the same analysis feel easier.

A public-product PM would ask for the behavioral baseline against which adoption and retention can be evaluated. An enterprise leader would inspect the end-to-end operating process, including data preparation by analysts or operations teams that may be invisible to the AE.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want a workflow map with timing, role ownership, failure points, and the decision each step supported. Evidence that the designer retired specific documents or handoffs would make the causal mechanism credible. If the baseline was reconstructed only from self-report after launch, I would treat the efficiency outcome as directional rather than proven.

## Answer

The baseline was observational, and The relevant detail is its texture and its limits. I shadowed the twice-weekly ritual directly with three AE–BDR pairs — sat in on their Monday and Thursday prep sessions over two weeks — and did artifact walkthroughs of the documents themselves with two more AEs from the interview set. The artifact was the real evidence. One mid-market AE with roughly 340 accounts ran a 27-column spreadsheet: exported account list, manually pasted consumption numbers from one dashboard, support-ticket counts eyeballed from another, a hand-typed "priority" column, a color code only she and her BDR understood, and a "notes" column that was effectively their CRM. The BDR's Monday job was reconciling the export against last week's copy — new accounts, ownership changes, term starts — before the AE would even look at it. The reconciliation step is where errors lived: I watched one session where a renewal-risk account had silently dropped off the sheet because an ownership change re-keyed the row.

The steps decomposed consistently across pairs: export and reconcile (BDR, one to two hours), paste and refresh metrics (BDR, another hour or so), scan for anomalies (AE, an hour), re-prioritize and annotate (both, the Thursday session). When I asked each pair to estimate weekly totals, self-reports ranged from three to seven hours per person depending on book size and quarter timing — heavier at quarter boundaries. I recorded those as informal tallies in my notes, not as measurements. I never ran an instrumented time study, and the later 4–6-hour survey figure rests on the same self-report methodology; the observation gave me confidence in the mechanism, not the number.

What the observation bought that a survey couldn't was the failure-point map, and two design decisions trace straight to it. First, the reconciliation pain is why Book of Business is account-owner-scoped with Term Start as a required filter: the tool's opening state answers "what changed in my book recently" — the exact question the BDR spent Monday rebuilding — and evaluates each customer relative to Day 0 of their contract instead of calendar weeks, which is how the pairs actually thought about progress. Second, the anomaly-scan pain became the six trend charts with the countdown header; "24 days remain in Q1FY24" is the ambient fact that drove every Thursday conversation I watched.

The alternative on baselining was to run a proper diary study across ten-plus pairs and get a defensible pre-launch distribution. I chose not to spend the time — the research window was borrowed and I judged three shadowed pairs plus artifact walkthroughs sufficient to design from. Banking that baseline would have strengthened the eventual efficiency claim; the absence of a hard pre-product baseline is now the weakest joint in the outcome story.

The work did not disappear symmetrically. The BDR's reconciliation labor was largely absorbed by the tool; the AE's judgment work remained, just faster. And I observed at least one pair keep a slimmed spreadsheet after launch for pipeline-review formatting reasons — the tool ate the analysis, not every artifact downstream of it.
