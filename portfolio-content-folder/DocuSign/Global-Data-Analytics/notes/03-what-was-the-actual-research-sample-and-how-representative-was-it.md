# 3. What was the actual research sample and how representative was it?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Provenance, defensibility, narrative command

**Question:** How many AEs, BDRs, PMs, managers, and data partners participated in interviews, workshops, focus groups, or post-release surveys; which segments, regions, verticals, and book sizes did they represent; how were they recruited; and what important differences or dissenting views did you find?

**Reason for investigation:** The Apple Note says “a few” employees were recruited, “a lot” of workshops were conducted, and different PMs helped map the tool landscape. The case later generalizes from those inputs to account populations ranging from fewer than 50 to as many as 3,000 customers. The research photographs add provenance texture, but participant count, selection method, role mix, and saturation are absent. The focus group that rejected the performance-comparison concept is also described only as small.

This matters because the products encode a model of how AEs prioritize and how customers should be compared. A public-product PM would want confidence that the design did not overfit enthusiastic participants. An enterprise systems leader would expect meaningful differences between strategic, enterprise, mid-market, and high-volume coverage models.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would provide the sample, explain unavoidable biases, identify findings that varied by segment, and show how the design handled those differences. I also want one instance where research contradicted a sponsor or the designer's own assumption. If the evidence came from a narrow convenience sample and was generalized without qualification, I would discount the claimed scale and defensibility.

## Answer

The sample was eighteen account executives, five product managers, and the core data team, and I'll break down what each actually did. Five of the eighteen AEs sat for generative interviews — sixty-to-seventy-five-minute sessions on how they work a book: how they organize, what they check Monday morning, how they decide who gets called. The remaining thirteen were recruited into focus-group activities, run as three sessions of four, five, and four, which is where the two Feature Usage Baseline interpretations were later tested. The five PMs were interviewed separately and differently — not about their roadmaps but about how metrics were helping them, which is where the tool-landscape mapping came from. The build collaboration was a distinct group: four product managers, one director of product management, and three data analysts/engineers.

Recruiting ran through the sales-ops manager, and that is the first bias to name: it was a warm-intro sample. AEs who respond to a sales-ops ask about "a research thing on dashboards" skew toward the engaged and the vocal. Segment coverage was mid-market heavy — most participants held books in the 50-to-200 range, a few above that, and only two I'd genuinely call enterprise/strategic with books under fifty. The most consequential gap: the problem statement cites Mid-Market-to-Virtual AEs managing 200 to 3,000 customers, and nobody I interviewed personally managed near the top of that range. The high-volume end of the design was extrapolated from the mid-market pattern plus the sales-ops manager's descriptions, not observed directly. That's why the Term Start filter exists — the required last-45-days window was my structural answer to books too large to render — but I validated it against a 200-account book, not a 2,000-account one.

Saturation was real on the core finding. By the fourth or fifth AE, the pattern held: everyone claimed a unique process, and everyone's process reduced to a handful of metrics used in a personal configuration. Six AEs gave me articulable bucketing heuristics — MRR-and-usage quadrants, product-centered isolation, support-ticket watching — and those six became the raw material for the segmentation model.

Dissent existed and mattered. One enterprise AE told me flatly that bucket models were irrelevant at thirty accounts — "I know all of my customers by name; I don't need a filter to find them" — which is why the published scope statement says the tools mainly target AEs with more than fifty accounts. That was research contradicting my own early assumption that one segmentation surface could serve every band. A second dissent came from a BDR who sat in on one interview: she pointed out that half the spreadsheet ritual existed because the AE didn't trust the dashboard's refresh timing, which pushed me to put "Last refreshed" timestamps prominently on both shipped headers.

Eighteen AEs is a defensible qualitative base for pattern-finding and a thin base for the generalization the products encode. Regional and vertical spread was whatever the warm-intro network produced — I did not stratify it. And the focus group that killed the performance-comparison concept was nine people across two of the three sessions. I treated it as decisive because the objection was structural rather than preferential, but I cannot claim it was a powered study. It was a small, well-run, biased sample that found one real thing and I built on that thing.
