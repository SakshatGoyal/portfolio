# 9. Which impact figures existed during the project, and which were modeled later for the portfolio?

**Category:** Missing provenance and potentially misleading scope  
**Caliber markers:** Provenance, consequence, narrative command

**Question:** The supplemental report models 760 in-scope AEs, $1.77B of revenue under coverage, and $29.2M–$65.7M of annual capacity value, while clearly labeling these as assumptions. Which inputs were known to the product team at the time, which were reconstructed later, and how would you prevent a reader from mistaking operating footprint or modeled capacity for realized impact?

**Reason for investigation:** The report is unusually careful about the difference between observed outcomes, designed targets, modeled value, and revenue context. That honesty is good. But the scale figures are built from analyst assumptions about AE headcount, role mix, coverage, adoption, hourly value, and a one-to-one AE–BDR pairing. They are not product outcomes. Their numerical size can dominate the story even though four critical inputs are unverified.

A public-product PM will penalize exposure presented as impact. An enterprise leader may appreciate scenario planning but will ask for licensed users, active adoption, internal labor costs, and realized redeployment. At Staff level, the designer must demonstrate metric judgment, not metric spectacle.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want a clean evidence timeline and the exact claims the designer would make in a live interview. A strong answer would lead with the observed workflow change, use the model only as a planning bridge, and state what data is needed to validate it. If the designer leans on the large modeled figures to imply Staff-level consequence, my confidence in the story would fall.

## Answer

Clean timeline, because this is where a portfolio can quietly launder a model into an outcome. Known to the team during the project: the account counts and coverage bands as rough operating knowledge (that the tools targeted AEs above ~50 accounts; that mid-market books ran 200-plus; the dashboard's own 207-account example was sized from a real mid-market book shape); the post-release survey and its 4–6-hour compression; the qualitative reports of snapshots appearing in customer decks; and the threshold-variation capture as an emerging dataset. That is the complete list. Nobody during the project computed 760 in-scope AEs, $1.77B revenue under coverage, or $29.2M–$65.7M of annual capacity. No such figures appeared in any artifact I made at DocuSign.

Those numbers were constructed in July 2026 for the portfolio's supplemental report, and they are explicitly a model: public FY2026 company revenue, an analyst-assumed 1,000-AE global force, an assumed 80/20 allocation, assumed band splits (240 enterprise, 390 mid-market, 370 SMB), an assumed one-to-one AE–BDR pairing, and assumed loaded hourly values. Four critical inputs — licensed users, active-user rate, BDR ratio, sustained time saving — are flagged in the report itself as unverified. The report exists because I wanted to answer "what could this be worth if the reported mechanism held at scale" with disciplined arithmetic instead of hand-waving, and because modeling it with explicit assumptions seemed more useful than leaving a vacuum a reader would fill with worse assumptions.

How I prevent a reader from mistaking footprint for impact: layered claims, in order, with the confidence stated. In a live interview I lead with the observed workflow change — a real twice-weekly ritual, shadowed, decomposed, and removed for paired roles — then the reported outcome with its self-report caveats, then the behavioral dataset as delivered capability, and only then, if asked about scale, the model, introduced with the words "here is a planning scenario built on analyst assumptions, not a result." The scenario-bridge table in the report (basis-point sensitivities on covered revenue) is the same discipline: it exists to show what instrumentation could someday substantiate, and it is labeled never to be presented as realized revenue.

The contested decision here was whether to include the modeled figures in the portfolio at all. The alternative — leave them out entirely and let the case rest on the survey and the qualitative evidence — was argued to me by a mentor reviewing the case, on the grounds that big numbers gravitationally dominate careful labels; readers remember $45.6M and forget "modeled." I chose inclusion with aggressive labeling, because the alternative failure mode is real too: an internal-tools case with no economic frame reads as unsized, and evaluators fill the vacuum with "probably small." I may still be wrong about which failure mode costs more.

What I'd need to convert the model into a claim: licensed and active user counts, instrumented time-in-workflow before and after, the actual BDR support ratio, and one credible cohort comparison — phased rollout or matched books. None of that data reached me before the engagement ended. So the durable answer is: everything that sounds like measurement was measured; everything that sounds like scale was modeled; and the report's own evidence register is the contract that keeps those categories from bleeding into each other.
