# 8. How credible is the reported 4–6 hours saved per AE and per BDR each week?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Consequence, provenance, defensibility

**Question:** How many users reported the 4–6-hour saving, when and how were they surveyed, what was the response rate and distribution, was the question anchored to a pre-launch baseline, and did telemetry or observation confirm that old spreadsheets and documents were actually abandoned?

**Reason for investigation:** This is the clearest outcome in the entire case and therefore the claim most likely to be probed in an interview. The supplemental evidence register correctly admits recall bias, unknown AE–BDR ratios, and the possibility of quiet abandonment. The portfolio wording, however, presents the saving as a straightforward product outcome. Four to six hours for each of two roles every week is large enough that I would expect strong measurement and visible workflow retirement.

A public-product PM would ask about active users, sustained use, cohort retention, and causal attribution. An enterprise leader would ask whether work moved to Sales Ops, data engineering, or managers and whether recovered time was used productively.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would provide sample size, distribution rather than only a range, time horizon, comparison method, active-use evidence, and the specific tasks eliminated. It would use careful language such as “reported capacity” rather than payroll savings. If the designer cannot explain how the number was produced, the claim would reduce trust in the rest of the case.

## Answer

I'll give the mechanics as precisely as memory and notes allow, because the number deserves its asterisks. The survey was a post-release pulse run through the sales-ops manager a couple of months after Book of Business reached the mid-market teams. I drafted two of the questions; sales-ops owned distribution and shielded respondent identity. The time question asked for the current weekly hours spent on organizing account data and prioritization documents, with a recalled-estimate of the same before the tool — which is to say, both ends of the delta were self-reported, and the "before" end was recalled after the fact. It was not anchored to my pre-launch shadowing notes, because those covered three pairs, not the respondent base.

Response volume was modest — my recollection is roughly two dozen responses from the sixty-odd people with access at the time, AEs and BDRs mixed, and the AE-to-BDR ratio among respondents was not captured, which the later evidence register correctly flags. The distribution, not just the range: most respondents clustered in the three-to-six-hour band, a few claimed more, a couple claimed near zero — those last being, as far as I could tell from sales-ops's characterization, people who had barely adopted the tool. "4–6 hours per AE and per BDR" was the product team's compression of that distribution's center. I'd have reported it as "respondents typically reported 3–6 hours, self-estimated" — the published phrasing is rounder and more confident than the instrument justifies, and I've adopted the discipline of saying "reported capacity" rather than savings.

Telemetry confirmation of abandonment: none existed. The old artifacts were personal spreadsheets and docs on individual drives; there was no way to instrument their retirement, and we didn't try. My confirming evidence is observational and small: of the three pairs I had shadowed pre-launch, I followed up informally with two. One had genuinely retired the reconciliation ritual — the BDR's Monday block was gone. The other kept a slimmed sheet because their pipeline-review format demanded a spreadsheet artifact; the tool fed it, so the analysis time fell while the artifact survived. That second case is the shape I'd bet is most common: work reduced and displaced, not eliminated. Whether some respondents quietly reverted entirely, the survey cannot reveal — abandoners don't answer pulse surveys about a tool they stopped using.

Was work shifted to other roles? Partially, and it's material: the tool's nightly extracts leaned on pipelines the data engineers now maintained. That's a deliberate trade — centralized maintenance replacing distributed manual labor is the whole point — but a full accounting would net the data team's ongoing cost against the field's recovered hours, and no one did that accounting.

So my credibility position, stated the way I'd state it to a skeptical panel: the mechanism is solid — I watched the specific hours the tool targets being spent, step by step, before it existed. The direction is well-supported — a self-selected but real respondent set reported large reductions, consistent with what the mechanism predicts. The magnitude is directional — self-reported, recall-anchored, from a modest sample, without abandonment telemetry. I'd stake my name on "this tool removed a real, recurring, multi-hour ritual for paired roles." I would not stake it on the integer six.
