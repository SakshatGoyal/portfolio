# 13. How was the feature taxonomy by customer value created, governed, and reused?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Force multiplication, decisions, attribution

**Question:** You reorganized DocuSign features from plan-based groupings into groups based on the value they offered customers. Who defined and approved those value propositions, how were overlapping features handled, how did the taxonomy stay current as packaging changed, and did Product, Marketing, Enablement, or other dashboards adopt it?

**Reason for investigation:** This may be a more important Staff-level artifact than the dashboard itself. A cross-product value taxonomy can align sales conversations, product analytics, packaging, and customer outcomes. Yet the case presents it as an individual design step, supported by a sticky-note workshop image, without showing collaborators, disagreements, governance, or downstream adoption. “Value” can easily become vague marketing language unless it is tied to measurable customer outcomes.

A public-product PM would want to know whether the taxonomy changed positioning or prioritization. An enterprise leader would ask how a cross-functional classification became a maintained system rather than a one-time workshop output.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would name the cross-functional owners, contested classifications, maintenance process, and artifacts or teams that reused the model. That would be direct force-multiplication evidence. If the grouping existed only inside one dashboard, it remains a useful information-architecture decision but not a Staff-level organizational system.

## Answer

The taxonomy was built in one long working session and refined over about two weeks, and the sticky-note wall photo — the one shot at night against the conference-room glass — is the actual artifact mid-flight. The method: every DocuSign feature went up as a note; the existing organization was plan-based (which features come with Standard, Business Pro, Enterprise Pro), and the reorganization axis was "what would an account team be selling when they sell this feature." Eight value groups came out and survived to the shipped left-nav: Enhanced Document Customization, Efficient Sending Experience, Improved Signer Experience, Internal Activities and Collaboration, Integrations, Maintain Legal & Security Compliance, Controlled Sending Experience, and APIs.

Who was in the room: me driving, with two of the four product managers, and a review pass afterward with a product marketing manager the director pulled in. The PMs supplied feature-behavior ground truth; the marketing reviewer sanity-checked that group names read as sellable value rather than internal taxonomy. Approval was working-level — the director blessed it as the dashboard's organizing scheme; no packaging or pricing authority ever ratified it, and I'll come back to why that matters.

The contested classifications were the most instructive part. Bulk Send: efficiency feature or control feature? It ended in Efficient Sending Experience alongside PowerForms, Template, and Web Forms, but one PM argued hard for Controlled Sending because enterprise buyers acquire it for governed mass-distribution. SMS Delivery and WhatsApp Delivery: sending efficiency or signer experience? They landed in Improved Signer Experience with Delegated Signing, Elastic Signing, and Mobile Sign and Return — the deciding argument was whose life improves, and delivery channels improve the signer's, which also set up the eventual outcome line about SMS Delivery and completion time. The general rule we settled: classify by the beneficiary of the value proposition, not by the mechanism. Overlapping features — genuinely multi-value ones like PowerForms, which is arguably an API story too — got a single primary home anyway, because the taxonomy's job was navigation and narrative, and a feature that lives in two nav groups teaches users the groups mean nothing. Single-homing was a lossy compression I chose consciously; the alternative, primary-plus-secondary tagging, I rejected for the shipped surface and would revisit for any analytical reuse.

The taxonomy was useful but never governed. The taxonomy lived in exactly two places: the FUB left-nav and a mapping sheet I maintained. There was no owner designated after me, no process wired to packaging changes — when product marketing repackaged plans, nothing forced a taxonomy review, so drift was a matter of time. Downstream adoption during my window: the Features Used filter in Book of Business consumed the same grouping, which is real but in-family reuse; the product marketing reviewer told me the value-group framing was useful for enablement material she was drafting, which I never verified landed. Product analytics, marketing dashboards, enablement curricula — none adopted it while I could see. So by the question's own standard: as shipped, this was a strong information-architecture decision with one adjacent reuse, not a maintained organizational system. I think it was the most force-multipliable artifact in the project — a value-based feature ontology is exactly the kind of thing sales, product, and marketing could have converged on — and its failure to institutionalize is the clearest case of my contractor scope ending before the system-building work that would have earned the larger claim.
