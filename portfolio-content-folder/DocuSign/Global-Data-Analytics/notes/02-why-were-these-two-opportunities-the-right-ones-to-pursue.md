# 2. Why were these two opportunities the right ones to pursue?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Contested decisions, problem altitude, consequence

**Question:** Research appears to have surfaced many possible ways to make dashboards more action-oriented. What opportunity set did you create, what criteria did you use to select Book of Business and Feature Usage Baseline, what did you explicitly deprioritize, and how did expected user value, technical feasibility, data readiness, and company strategy affect the choice?

**Reason for investigation:** The story moves from broad workshops and landscape review to two hypotheses, but it does not show the portfolio decision between them and other possibilities. The two selected products address different moments: one organizes an AE's entire account portfolio, while the other supports feature discovery and customer persuasion. That breadth could indicate excellent opportunity framing, or it could mean the story combines two unrelated dashboards without showing why they deserved investment.

At Staff level, prioritization is part of the design contribution. A public-product PM would expect an opportunity-sizing and sequencing argument. An enterprise leader would ask whether both products used shared data infrastructure and whether pursuing them together created leverage or competing governance burdens.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want the rejected opportunities, selection criteria, sequencing, and the evidence that made one hypothesis more urgent or tractable. A strong answer might show that one product generated learning or infrastructure for the other. If the products were simply the concepts the designer was personally most interested in, I would question the product-management judgment beneath the design initiative.

## Answer

The research surfaced three options. One was Book of Business — the twice-weekly AE–BDR spreadsheet ritual was the loudest, most concrete pain in every workshop. The second was the peer-cohort hypothesis, which had two contrasting interpretations — compare a customer's performance metrics against a cohort, or compare what a customer purchased and what it did for them — and only later became Feature Usage Baseline. The third was a renewal-risk early-warning concept for customer success managers, built off the support-ticket-centered patterns some AEs described. On pure user value I thought the third was arguably the strongest; churn risk is where the money is.

We pursued the two sales-facing options, and the deciding variable was the sponsor, not the sizing. The director, despite covering multiple data verticals, had a visible preference toward Sales — his relationships were there, his wins were counted there. The CSM concept meant negotiating with the customer-success org, whose leadership had its own analytics ambitions and a history of friction with the data team; the marketing- and PM-facing ideas raised the same class of cross-org objections. The sales options had a warm sponsor, willing participants, and the fewest veto-holders. My reasoning was sequencing: get two quick wins inside the sponsor's zone of enthusiasm, build momentum and reusable infrastructure, then come back for the harder cross-org opportunities from a position of demonstrated value. Choosing the CSM concept would have made this a more cross-organizational, more Staff-shaped problem, but also a slower and riskier one. I chose the beachhead.

The second half of the sequencing theory never executed. The momentum was real — BoB's reception genuinely made the FUB conversation easier — but the third option was never revived. By the time both tools shipped, my triage bandwidth was consumed, the CSM org had started its own health-score initiative without design involvement, and the window closed. So "we'll get to it once we have momentum" turned out to be the story I told myself about a decision that was substantially about the director's favoritism. I knew that at the time; I judged that riding the preference was better than fighting it, because fighting it likely meant no research program at all. I still think that trade was right. I don't think I get to also claim the third option as deferred rather than lost.

Within the two pursued options, the selection criteria were real and I can name them: data readiness (the Alation schemas showed use-case and sub-industry data already being collected — FUB needed no new pipelines; BoB needed only consolidation of metrics I had just migrated, so the Tableau work was literally the infrastructure), observable pain frequency (twice a week, every week, for BoB), and testability (both could be evaluated with the same 18-AE pool I had already recruited). There was also genuine leverage between them: BoB taught us how AEs segment and prioritize; FUB's cohort logic reused the same Salesforce attribute spine, and the focus groups for one seeded the participants for the other. That part of the portfolio argument holds. The order of causation matters: the director's interest picked the lane, and the criteria then did real work inside it. If the interviewer's concern is whether I exercised independent product judgment or optimized for sponsorship — the answer is both, deliberately, and in that order for one round only.
