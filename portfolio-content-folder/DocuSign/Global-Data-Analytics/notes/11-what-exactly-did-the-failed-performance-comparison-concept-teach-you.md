# 11. What exactly did the failed performance-comparison concept teach you?

**Category:** The visible decision is strong but incomplete  
**Caliber markers:** Contested decisions, provenance, defensibility

**Question:** When the focus group rejected or distrusted the performance-benchmark interpretation, what did participants actually say, how many reacted that way, which customer-risk assumptions did you identify, and why did that evidence justify pivoting toward purchase-and-outcome comparisons rather than abandoning peer benchmarking entirely?

**Reason for investigation:** This is the case's best demonstrated decision: two plausible interpretations were designed, and testing revealed that AEs evaluated data through the lens of what they could credibly show a customer. The performance version risked implying that DocuSign had not been a fair or effective partner. That is specific and consequential. What is missing is the depth behind the pivot. A small focus group can reveal a risk without proving the alternative safe or valuable.

A public-product PM would ask whether the new concept solved the customer job or merely felt easier to sell. An enterprise leader would ask how legal, customer success, product analytics, and data governance participated in the decision. At Staff level, the designer should show not only the insight but the decision boundary it created.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want the participant evidence, the rejected screens, the stakeholders involved, and the criteria for continuing. A strong answer would also name the contexts in which performance benchmarking remained useful internally. If the pivot was based on a few favorable reactions to sales-oriented data, I would see good discovery instincts but insufficient validation.

## Answer

The two interpretations were both designed to comparable fidelity — that matters, because the focus groups reacted to real screens, not a described idea. The performance version showed a customer's send velocity, completion rate, and time-to-completion against a similar-customer cohort; the purchase version showed feature adoption against the cohort. Nine AEs across two focus sessions saw both. My tally, recorded in the session notes as an informal count: seven of the nine raised some version of the same objection against the performance version, unprompted, and — the detail that made it decisive for me — the objection was not "this isn't useful." It was "what happens when the customer sees this." One AE said almost exactly: if I show an account their completion time is worse than their peers', the customer hears that DocuSign has been letting them fail. Another said she would never bring a slide like that into a QBR, and then — this was the tell — asked whether she could bring the other one.

That reframe is the entire lesson: the AEs evaluated an internal analytics surface as customer-facing collateral, even though the design intent was private analysis. When AEs look at a customer's metrics, they almost never see private information; they see a future slide. The performance version failed not on comprehension or accuracy but on the anticipated social meaning of the number in front of a customer — a benchmark that makes the customer look worse than peers indicts the vendor as much as the customer.

Why pivot within peer benchmarking rather than abandon it? Because the objection was precise, and precision is directional. The rejected thing was performance comparison shown to customers; the demanded thing surfaced in the same sessions — AEs reported that customers actively ask what their peers purchase. The H&M example came from a participant: an H&M wants to know what Bloomingdale's and Zara buy from DocuSign, as both benchmark and onboarding shortcut. So the same cohort machinery, pointed at purchases and their associated outcomes instead of at raw performance, passed the exact test the first version failed: it's a comparison an AE can defend in front of the customer. That sentence became the design's stated rationale and drove the comparison-criteria architecture — context-specific defaults from the account's own Salesforce attributes, with AE override, because an indefensible cohort is a useless one.

What justified continuing was cheap validation stacked in the pivot's favor: the second design already existed, the demand signal (customers asking about peers) was reported independently by multiple participants, and one focus group's reaction was strong enough that participants asked for the tool with imperfect data — an adoption signal I logged while also logging it as a risk, since customer-facing comparisons are precisely where imperfect data is dangerous.

Important limits: Performance benchmarking wasn't abandoned as information — a customer's own performance trends stayed in Book of Business, scoped to the AE's private view, never cohort-compared; the ban was on peer-relative performance in anything snapshot-able. Legal and customer-success were not in the focus-group loop; the decision boundary was drawn from user evidence, and formal review of customer-facing claim language never happened at concept stage — that gap resurfaces in the causality question and the responsibility is mine. And nine participants revealed a risk and a direction; they did not prove the purchase version commercially valuable. The pivot was well-evidenced discovery. Validation of the destination remained thinner than I'd have liked all the way to ship.
