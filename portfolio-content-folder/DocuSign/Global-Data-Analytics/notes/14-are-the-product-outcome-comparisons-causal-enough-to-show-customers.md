# 14. Are the product-outcome comparisons causal enough to show customers?

**Category:** The work is understandable but unconvincing and potentially risky  
**Caliber markers:** Consequence, product judgment, defensibility

**Question:** The interface makes statements such as an account's envelopes taking one-third longer to complete than customers with SMS delivery. How did you distinguish correlation from the causal effect of a feature, control for customer and workflow differences, and ensure Sales did not imply that buying the feature would produce the observed outcome?

**Reason for investigation:** Moving from “peers bought this” to “peers achieved this outcome” makes the evidence more useful—and much more dangerous. Customers who purchased a feature may differ in maturity, use case, scale, implementation quality, or support level. A median and range do not resolve selection bias. The artifacts use persuasive customer-facing language but do not show caveats, methodology, confidence, or approval from Legal and Product Analytics.

A public-product PM would consider customer trust, claims substantiation, and the possibility of a dark pattern in value-led selling. An enterprise leader would ask how outcome definitions were governed and reproduced. At Staff level, the designer should have shaped the ethical and evidentiary boundary, not only the presentation.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would describe the analysis method, approved wording, caveats, review partners, and situations where only association could be stated. It might show that the dashboard was an internal prompt for investigation rather than a customer promise. If the designer accepted causal-sounding language because it helped sales, I would significantly weaken my confidence in the designer’s judgment.

## Answer

No — and the craft position I held was that they didn't need to be causal, they needed to describe the association accurately, with that limit carried in the sentence structure. The mechanism: the Feature Impact chart plots the selected account's metric against the median and extents of similar accounts that use the feature. That is an observed association between feature adoption and outcome within a matched-attribute cohort. It controls for what the cohort attributes control for — region, segment, industry, sub-industry, department, use-case — and nothing else. Customers who bought SMS Delivery may differ in implementation maturity, workflow design, volume, support tier; a median and a range do not touch selection bias, and I said so in the design reviews in roughly those words.

Wording was the only boundary I could enforce directly. The canonical line is "your envelopes take 1/3 more time to complete than customers who have SMS delivery." Note what it doesn't say: not "because they lack SMS delivery," not "SMS delivery will cut your completion time by a third." The construction is a group-membership comparison — customers *with* the feature versus you — which is factually defensible from the data, where the causal phrasing would not be. I wrote a short do/don't sheet of claim patterns to accompany the design: compare against the cohort, never promise the delta; the chart legend's own vocabulary ("Median for Similar Accounts using feature," "Extents") was chosen to keep the surface's language observational. The Salesforce-data PM reviewed the wording patterns with me. Legal did not — there was no claims-substantiation review in the loop for an internal tool, even one whose snapshots we knew were headed into customer decks, and I flagged that asymmetry in writing when the snapshot-in-decks behavior was first reported. An internal tool's compliance posture attached to an increasingly external artifact is a real governance hole; naming it was as far as my leverage reached.

The residual risk I couldn't design away is the human one: the AE in the room can convert association to promise with their voice, chart notwithstanding. Two mitigations existed, one structural, one aspirational. Structural: the criteria-adjacent-to-chart rule meant any snapshot carried its cohort definition, so a customer's own analysts could interrogate the comparison — the artifact invites scrutiny rather than defying it. Aspirational: I proposed enablement guidance for account teams on presenting baselines — the "this is what similar customers experience, let's discuss whether it applies to you" framing — which is investigation-prompting rather than outcome-promising. That guidance was drafted, never formally rolled out while I was there.

The alternative was to keep outcome comparisons internal-only and let snapshots carry purchase-adoption data alone — peers bought this, at these levels — with outcomes reserved for the AE's private preparation. It was the conservative option. I chose to expose outcomes because the focus groups showed the outcome connection was what made conversations less misleading in the other direction: pure purchase comparison invites "everyone buys it" herd selling, while outcome framing at least ties the pitch to something the customer can experience and later verify. I still think that trade was right. But asked directly whether the shipped comparisons were causal enough to support the strongest way an AE could use them — no. They were defensible as evidence of association, dependent on wording discipline and cohort transparency, and one enthusiastic AE away from overclaim at any given QBR. That is the true state, and the case is more trustworthy stated that way.
