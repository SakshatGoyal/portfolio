# 7. How did you balance AE customization with organizational consistency?

**Category:** The work is understandable but raises concern  
**Caliber markers:** Decisions, consequence, enterprise judgment

**Question:** The product lets each AE change the MRR threshold and other criteria, while the organization can observe how thresholds vary by region, vertical, book size, and quarter. What was configurable, what remained governed, how were defaults set, and how did you prevent local optimization, inconsistent customer treatment, or threshold changes made to serve quota optics rather than sound account strategy?

**Reason for investigation:** The case correctly observes that each AE's method varies, and the adjustable threshold is a compelling simulation tool. But the supplemental impact note treats “default divergence” and “definition persistence” as success measures without evidence that an off-default threshold is better. Capturing variation can produce organizational learning; it can also normalize inconsistent prioritization or turn a dashboard into an instrument of performance surveillance.

A public-product PM would ask whether customization improved outcomes and retention. An enterprise product leader would worry about governance, comparability, incentives, and trust between sellers and operations. At Staff level, the designer should show where flexibility ends and why.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would explain default-setting, validation against downstream outcomes, manager visibility, change history, and guardrails against gaming. It would distinguish healthy adaptation from arbitrary divergence. If the product rewarded customization simply because customization occurred, I would question the metric design and operating-model judgment.

## Answer

The configurability boundary was drawn narrowly and deliberately. What an AE could change: the MRR threshold (a free text field, $1,000 default), every filter selection, the trend-line pivot, and the view toggle. What they could not change: the six bucket definitions themselves, the metric definitions behind the charts, and the required-ness of Term Start. The philosophy was: thresholds are opinions, definitions are infrastructure. Every AE I interviewed had an opinion on what counted as high MRR relative to their quarter, region, and objective, independent of internal guidelines — that's written verbatim on the design annotation — so locking the threshold would have made the tool wrong for most of its users on day one. But if bucket logic or metric definitions had been editable, two AEs looking at the same account could disagree about what "Analysis Segment" even means, and the model would have dissolved into per-seat vocabulary.

The $1,000 default was set from the observed center of the mid-market heuristics I collected — several of the interviewed AEs used round figures between $500 and $2,000, and $1,000 was the modal anchor. I documented it as a starting point for simulation, not a recommendation, and the threshold field is required-with-default rather than optional precisely so that changing it is a conscious act with a visible current value.

I did not include a manager-facing drill-down of individual AEs' thresholds. The organizational capture — thresholds by region, vertical, book size, quarter — was aggregate, and it flowed to the data team, not to sales management. I argued this explicitly in a working session with two of the PMs when one floated a "coaching view" of per-AE settings: the moment an AE believes their threshold choice is performance-visible, they'll set it to whatever looks diligent, and both the tool's integrity and the dataset's value die together. Managers could have used divergence to find coaching moments, but that would have required explicit guardrails. I judged the trust cost higher than the coaching value on a tool whose adoption was voluntary.

What prevented gaming toward quota optics? Structurally, less than the question deserves. The threshold changes nothing except the analytical split — it doesn't feed compensation, doesn't change account assignment, doesn't report upward — so the gaming incentive was low because the stakes were private. That's a direct answer but a fragile one: the moment anyone wires bucket counts into forecasting or QBR reporting, the threshold becomes an instrument worth manipulating, and the design has no audit history to catch it. I flagged threshold-change history as an instrumentation requirement; it was not built in the shipped version.

Nothing showed that an off-default threshold produced better account strategy than the default. Default divergence and definition persistence appear in the later measurement registry as designed targets exactly because they were never measured in deployment. At ship, customization was justified by fit-to-mental-model and by the simulation value AEs reported — iterate on your notion of high and low without rebuilding a spreadsheet — not by outcome evidence. Healthy adaptation versus arbitrary divergence remained an untested distinction the whole time I was there.
