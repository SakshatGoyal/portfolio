# 4. What did Workbench consolidation actually retire, simplify, or change organizationally?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Consequence, force multiplication, provenance

**Question:** You state that the Workbench let users perform smaller updates and analysis without visiting seven or eight tools, helped decommission dashboards, and became an umbrella for Account, Opportunity, Sales Play, Technical Seller, and other workbenches. Which tools or dashboards were retired, for how many users, what work moved into Workbench, what remained outside, and what measurable operating or maintenance benefit followed?

**Reason for investigation:** Tool consolidation is the platform context that makes Anchored Follow-Ups and Trace strategically relevant. The artifacts show a real fragmented landscape and a dense consolidated interface. But “successfully decommissioned different dashboards” is not specific enough to assess. Consolidation can reduce switching while also creating a larger, harder-to-govern monolith. It can move maintenance cost rather than eliminate it, and adoption may vary by role.

A public-product PM would ask whether the platform improved retention, frequency, task completion, and product strategy. An enterprise leader would ask about duplicate data pipelines, ownership, support burden, and migration. The Staff-level question is whether the designer helped create a durable platform direction across teams.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would name the retired surfaces by function, give before-and-after workflow and usage evidence, explain migration tradeoffs, and show the cross-team decision mechanism. If consolidation was primarily visual aggregation while source-system work persisted unchanged, I would moderate the platform-impact claim.

## Answer

I'll answer with the specific surfaces, because "decommissioned different dashboards" is doing too much work in the case study. Over the two quarters after the consolidation shipped (roughly November 2025 through spring 2026), three Tableau dashboards were formally retired: a renewals-risk dashboard maintained by sales ops, a sales-play adoption tracker maintained by the sales-plays PM team, and an account-health rollup that had two competing versions — retiring it meant retiring an argument, which is why it took a quarter longer than the other two. "Retired" means access removed and the refresh jobs stopped, which our data engineering lead insisted on, because a dashboard that still refreshes is a dashboard that still gets screenshotted into decks. Two more surfaces were reduced rather than retired: the Sales Play Console remained the system of record for play administration but lost its account-list browsing role to the Sales Play Workbench, and a pending-quote-approvals report survived because approvals carry an audit workflow we had no business rebuilding.

What moved into the Workbench was deliberately the small-update tier: renewal-outcome tagging, sales-play status changes, opportunity-type corrections, and the reading workflows around them — the seven-or-eight-tool morning circuit became browsable in one place across Account, Opportunity, and Sales Play Workbenches. What stayed outside, on purpose: quoting and CPQ entirely, forecast submission (Clari-side workflows), and anything with an approval chain. So the architectural description is: source systems kept their write authority for consequential transactions; the Workbench took reads and low-consequence writes through APIs. A skeptic calling that "visual aggregation plus a thin write layer" would be more right than wrong for the first release — the difference from a dashboard was the write layer and the object model, not the pixels.

The measurable operating benefit is thin. What I can point to: the retired dashboards' maintenance went to zero (the renewals dashboard alone had a standing weekly-refresh babysitting task in sales ops); the population with Workbench access was in the low thousands across the account-team roles, but I never had a clean licensed-versus-active split, and The record does not contain one. Task-level timing was never measured before-and-after — the consolidation predated the metrics discipline that came with the AI features, which is a sequencing mistake I'd correct if I did it again: we instrumented the interesting features and not the boring migration that justified them.

The cross-team mechanism was a monthly decommission review my lead PM chaired with sales ops and the owning PMs — the forum where "who screams if this dies" got answered. Friction: the fringe-team PMs whose tools were being absorbed had no incentive to cooperate, and the director had to make dashboard-retirement a stated goal in two teams' planning to unblock it. One migration went badly enough to name: we retired the sales-play tracker two weeks before a QBR cycle, and the play-adoption numbers people expected to pull weren't yet exposed in the Workbench's export; sales ops rebuilt them by hand that cycle, and I got the escalation email. We added an export-parity check to the retirement checklist after that — the checklist became the most durable governance artifact consolidation produced.
