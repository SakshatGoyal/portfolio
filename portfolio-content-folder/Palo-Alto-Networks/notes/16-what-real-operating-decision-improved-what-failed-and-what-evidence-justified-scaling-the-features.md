# 16. What real operating decision improved, what failed, and what evidence justified scaling the features?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Consequence, force multiplication, defensibility

**Question:** Beyond arc engagement, what account-team decision became faster, more accurate, or more coordinated because of Anchored Follow-Ups or Trace—for example slip-risk review, whitespace identification, renewal planning, or manager alignment? What metric did not improve, where did users abandon or distrust the experience, and what stop-or-scale criteria did you use? How did the reported usage spike, accelerated data-engineering work, default table pattern, and dashboard decommissioning connect to a durable organizational outcome?

**Reason for investigation:** The case offers several impact signals: 23% arc rate, 4.3-step depth, a snapshot request, faster data-engineering activity, a table pattern reused elsewhere, and decommissioned dashboards. None currently has the causal detail needed to show Staff-level consequence. The later COO-office report models possible capacity and revenue exposure but correctly says those are planning scenarios, not realized value. It recommends a controlled trial with resolution time, evidence reproducibility, abandonment, action, and economics as gates.

A public-product PM would want a clear behavioral and business outcome plus a lesson from failure. An enterprise product leader would want end-to-end team time, error, governance incidents, downstream action, and run cost. The Staff-level gate is whether the designer created a product and decision framework that made multiple teams better—not merely a compelling AI interaction.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would name a real workflow, baseline, outcome, guardrails, failed hypothesis, and leadership decision; distinguish observed results from proposed validation; and show which systems or practices persisted without the designer's continued involvement. If the answer remains centered on longer sessions and enthusiasm, I would see innovative senior-level craft but not proven Staff-level consequence or multiplication.

## Answer

The workflow where I can trace a real chain is Monday slip-risk review. Before: a district manager's prep circuit ran through the forecast grid, per-deal activity timelines, and call-transcript search — assembling "which Commit deals are soft and why" by hand. After the internal release: forecast question in the panel, anchored deep-dives on flagged deals, trace expanded, evidence screenshotted into the forecast-call thread. I verified this end to end for one manager and saw the same shape in the Slack behavior of roughly a dozen others (the twenty-seven-screenshot count from Q14). What improved, concretely: forecast-call challenges arrived pre-answered — the number came with its derivation attached — which the manager described as fewer "let me get back to you" items per call. What I cannot give you: elapsed-time measurement, error rates, or the end-to-end team-time accounting that would show whether her saved time reappeared as someone else's verification work. The evidence stops one instrumented workflow short of proof.

Three failures matter. Adoption concentrated: something like a fifth of enabled users generated most of the arcs, forecast-adjacent roles overwhelmingly, and whitespace/cross-sell — the scenario I personally expected to shine — underperformed; users ran the mapping query, got the answer, and rarely deepened, which suggests that workflow wanted a report, not a conversation, and partially falsifies my own thesis that cross-source synthesis naturally produces analytical chains. Early wrong-object anchoring on expansion steps produced repair sequences that inflated depth while representing friction (fixed by constraining anchorable object classes — engineering's Salesforce-ID restriction — but the early telemetry is polluted and I said so when reporting). And the measurement system itself failed its own standard: the guardrail metric was never computable, so the documented status of "did arcs resolve" is unknown — the failure I'd put first in any retrospective.

What justified continued investment was never the 23%/4.3 pair alone. The decision-grade evidence inside the org was the usage spike plus the screenshot behavior — users spontaneously carrying outputs into decision forums — and its concrete consequence was resequencing: the data-engineering lead pulled forward the work to expose People.ai transcripts and stage history as governed sources by roughly a quarter, because query demand was visibly outrunning source coverage. That was the leadership decision the numbers actually changed. Stop-or-scale criteria in my window were embarrassingly informal — "abandonment must not rise" written into a definitions doc without a pipeline to compute it; the COO-office review later formalized the right gates (resolution time, reproducibility, abandonment, downstream action, economics) and recommended conditional scale via controlled trial, and my position is that their framework is what we should have written on day one, and that 23% sitting two points under our own 25% target is precisely a fund-the-trial number, not a scale number.

What persisted without me. The cluster-tag table as the default data-dense pattern — two teams adopted it unprompted, then it entered the shared component set with governance notes; the retired dashboards stayed retired, and the decommission checklist outlived the project; the metric vocabulary (session, step, arc, follow-up versus expansion, snapshot) survives in PM documents I didn't write; and the abstract/tacit scoping board was reused in two later scoping decisions. That list is the force-multiplication claim at its demonstrated size: patterns and instruments other people used after I stopped touching them — while the flagship features themselves remained, at my exit, a well-evidenced hypothesis with a funded path to proof rather than a proven operating outcome.
