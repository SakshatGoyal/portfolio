# 12. Why was a four-to-seven-step trace the right level of explanation?

**Category:** The work is understandable but under-validated  
**Caliber markers:** Craft, contested decisions, defensibility

**Question:** What evidence led to a short ordered trace of four to seven steps, how did users distinguish evidence from inference and absence-of-evidence, and how did the design support summary, drill-down, comparison, challenge, and correction? What alternative explanation structures were tested?

**Reason for investigation:** The research insight is credible: exposing a meandering model process does not help, while users need a scannable conclusion-derivation path. The trace screens show useful elements—querying pipeline, engagement signals, forecast checks, cross-referenced MEDDPICC fields, missing economic-buyer participation, numbered sources, and timestamps. Still, the number of steps appears arbitrary, and a neat linear narrative can oversimplify a branching or contradictory evidence base. It may imply causality where the system only found correlation.

A public-product PM would ask about comprehension, confidence calibration, and correction behavior. An enterprise leader would ask whether experts can reproduce, dispute, or annotate each claim. At Staff level, the designer should demonstrate that testing changed the information structure, not simply that the final trace is visually clean.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want comparative prototypes, user comprehension findings, examples of contradictory evidence, and the rules for labeling inference, missing data, and out-of-scope judgment. A strong answer would show that brevity was a tested tradeoff with progressive disclosure. If the step count came from aesthetic preference, the central verification claim remains under-developed.

## Answer

The range came from listening to how people defended numbers before we built anything. In the research walkthroughs I asked managers and AEs to justify a forecast position out loud, and their natural verbal derivations ran three to six assertions: pulled this, checked that, cross-referenced these, found this gap, therefore. Nobody defended a number in twelve moves, and nobody trusted a defense of one. That gave me the hypothesis that the trace should match the granularity of a human evidence argument, not the granularity of the system's actual operations — the orchestration log for a slip-risk question could contain twenty-plus operations, and the design question was how to collapse them into steps a skeptical reader audits in one pass.

I tested the collapse level directly, though at prototype scale, not as a comprehension study. Three variants of the slip-risk trace went in front of six users in moderated sessions: a three-step version (aggressive collapse), the seven-step version, and a twelve-step version exposing near-raw operations. The twelve-step version failed in a specific, instructive way — participants said it read like "thinking" again; two scrolled past it entirely, which is the exact behavior trace existed to fix. The three-step version read fine but failed the challenge test: when I asked "what would you check to disagree with this," participants couldn't attach their skepticism to a step — the collapse had swallowed the checkable claims. Seven steps survived both tests: scannable in one pass, and each step small enough to dispute individually. Four-to-seven then became a rendering rule, not an aesthetic: collapse the log to consequential operations, floor of four so a trace never looks like an assertion, ceiling of seven before the renderer must group operations under a parent step. The ceiling also fit the panel height at our default width — craft and constraint pointed the same direction, and I'd distrust anyone who claimed the pixel constraint played no role.

Distinguishing evidence from inference and absence: three step types in the schema. Retrieval steps state what was pulled and from where; inference steps state a comparison or ranking the system performed ("prioritized by close-date proximity"); absence steps state what was looked for and not found ("Found no evidence of Economic Buyer participation in last 30 days"), populated from result cardinality, not model phrasing. The absence type was the contested decision — my lead PM worried it read as accusation ("your deal has no champion") and proposed omitting negatives; I kept them because the walkthroughs showed missing evidence was precisely what managers probed for, and a trace silent on absences invites false confidence. Omitting negative evidence was viable, and keeping it carried a real cost: the harshest early feedback was an AE objecting that "no EB on a call" ignored an in-person meeting the system couldn't see — which is a data-coverage gap wearing the absence step's clothes, and led to scope statements ("In scope: … Out of scope: …") being appended to responses.

What I can't claim: a measured comprehension or confidence-calibration study, correction workflows (users could thumbs-down a trace but not annotate or dispute a specific step — spec'd, unbuilt), or a designed treatment for genuinely contradictory evidence beyond showing both sources with their freshness. Linearity oversimplifies branching evidence; for our two flagship workflows the evidence genuinely was mostly linear, and I'd expect the pattern to strain in workflows where it isn't.
