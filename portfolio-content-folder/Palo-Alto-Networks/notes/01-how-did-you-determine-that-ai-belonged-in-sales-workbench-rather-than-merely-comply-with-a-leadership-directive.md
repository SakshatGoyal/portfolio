# 1. How did you determine that AI belonged in Sales Workbench rather than merely comply with a leadership directive?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Problem altitude, contested decisions, provenance

**Question:** Leadership wanted an AI chatbot as part of the company's AI transition, while users were reportedly indifferent and preferred structured interfaces. What evidence and influence mechanism let you redefine the brief, which AI and non-AI alternatives were considered, what capability did you refuse to build, and what decision above your immediate team changed because of your argument?

**Reason for investigation:** The story contains a promising Staff-level tension: an executive push for a chatbot collided with experienced users' spreadsheet-native fluency and distrust of incorrect answers. The designer appears not to have rejected the mandate or accepted it literally; instead, the design preserved structured tables and used AI selectively for cross-source synthesis. That is good product instinct. What is missing is the organizational decision. I do not know whether leadership's directive was genuinely reframed, whether a PM had already made the same call, what alternative roadmap existed, or how much autonomy the designer held.

A public-product PM would evaluate whether the team found a real user problem rather than an AI use case. An enterprise product leader would also ask whether the designer protected high-consequence workflows from inappropriate automation. At Staff level, the case should show how the designer changed the “should and what” decision, not just how they improved the mandated chatbot.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would name the sponsor and dissenting stakeholders by role, show the option set, identify what was removed from the AI scope, and explain the artifact or prototype that changed leadership's decision. If the chatbot requirement remained fixed and the designer only shaped interaction details inside it, I would credit strong senior execution but not Staff-level problem reframing.

## Answer

The directive arrived in May 2025 with a sponsor attached: the VP of Sales Transformation owned the company's AI-transition OKR, and "conversational AI in the seller workflow" was a line item on it. The director of product for sales systems had already committed a chatbot milestone against that OKR before I was staffed. The decision that AI should exist had already been made above us. The negotiation I ran was over what shape of AI would count as delivering the OKR.

My influence mechanism had three parts. First, evidence: over June and July I ran conversations with account-team members across Americas, EMEA, and APJC, and the pattern was consistent — indifference to chat, deep fluency in structured tables, and a verification cost for wrong answers that outweighed the AI's benefit. Second, an artifact: in August I wrote a two-page AI scope memo whose core was the two-column classification — abstract unknowns (discovery, whitespace, forecast tracking) versus tacit tasks (quotation, product configuration) — with an explicit option set: (A) ship the general chatbot as committed; (B) ship consolidation only and defer AI; (C) bound AI to cross-source synthesis on abstract unknowns, with structured responses instead of narrative ones. Third, a demo: a clickable scenario showing option C answering a slip-risk question with a data table rather than prose paragraphs.

Option A remained viable — polish the mandated chatbot, ship the OKR, move on. The director of PM argued for exactly that in the August roadmap review, because option C put his committed milestone at risk of looking like a descope. The compromise that got option C through was partly mine and partly political: the VP needed a shippable AI story by fiscal year-end, and C was the only option that came with a working demo and a named list of workflows. We rewrote the milestone from "chatbot GA" to "AI-assisted analysis on three workflows," which is the decision above my team that changed because of the argument.

What I refused to build, on record in the memo: AI-generated quotations, product-configuration recommendations, autonomous writes to CRM fields or forecast categories, and a "daily AI insights digest" the director of PM wanted as a visible engagement play. The digest was the hardest refusal, because it was the cheapest to build and the best for adoption optics; I argued it was exactly the "insights without depth" pattern users had named, and it would train them to ignore the AI panel. I won that one; it cost me goodwill I later needed during the dashboard decommissioning fights.

The chatbot requirement was reframed, not rejected — the shipped AI panel is still a prompt box, and the tagline "Ask what usually takes four tools to answer" is a chat promise. Leadership accepted my framing partly because it served their optics, not purely because the evidence compelled them; if the VP hadn't needed a defensible AI narrative, I think option A ships. And I can't claim a PM hadn't been circling the same instinct — the lead PM on Workbench was sympathetic from the first review; what I supplied was the classification artifact and the prototype that made the reframe concrete enough to swap a milestone. The "should" decision stayed above me. The "what" decision is the one I genuinely changed.
