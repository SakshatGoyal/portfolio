# 11. Is Trace a real evidence trail or a model-written explanation that looks authoritative?

**Category:** The work is understandable but materially concerning  
**Caliber markers:** Product judgment, provenance, defensibility

**Question:** The Notion story says Trace breaks down “a model's thinking” into four to seven concrete steps, while the later operational analysis correctly says Trace should not expose chain-of-thought and must be generated from executed queries and data-lineage logs. Which architecture was actually built, which wording is accurate, and how did you prevent the model from inventing a plausible derivation after producing its conclusion?

**Reason for investigation:** This is the most important trust question in the project. The visual concept—conclusion first, concise evidence steps, numbered sources, and refresh times—is strong. It directly addresses the cost of verification. But a model can hallucinate an explanation as easily as an answer. If Trace is post-hoc narrative, the interface may launder uncertainty into false confidence precisely because it looks audited. The artifacts alternate among “thinking,” “derivation,” “decision trail,” and “trace,” which are not interchangeable concepts.

A public-product PM would treat fabricated citations as a severe trust and liability risk. An enterprise product leader would require reproducible queries, immutable logs, source permissions, and record-level deep links. At Staff level, the designer must be precise about the system boundary and should have challenged misleading language.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would describe the actual execution architecture, what was prototype simulation versus production behavior, how each displayed claim maps to a logged source operation, and how the team tested recomputability. If Trace was only a designed representation of model reasoning, I would consider it an unsafe concept until proven otherwise.

## Answer

The precise answer requires splitting three layers, because the artifacts blur them and the blurring is partly my fault. The Notion line "Trace breaks down a model's thinking into 4–7 concrete steps" is wrong as an architectural description — it's discovery-era language from when the feature was called Derivation and we were still contrasting it with chain-of-thought displays. I should have rewritten it when the architecture firmed up, and its survival into a published artifact is a real wording failure with real consequences, since "thinking" implies exactly the post-hoc rationale the design was built to avoid.

The three layers. In the design prototype, traces were authored: I wrote the step sequences for the demo scenarios — slip-risk (seven steps, six sources) and opportunity/platform mapping (six steps, two sources) — as typed TraceStep objects in scenario fixtures. Every design property (conclusion-first ordering, numbered steps, absence-of-evidence steps like "Found no evidence of Economic Buyer participation," source chips carrying dataset plus last-refresh) was real; the derivations themselves were simulated. Nobody inside the team was misled about this, but the case-study reader could be, and that prototype-versus-production distinction matters.

In the internal release, the trace was generated from the orchestration log, and the distinction matters enough to state carefully. The system answered questions by executing a plan of tool calls — retrieval against Salesforce forecast categories, stage history, notes, People.ai transcripts, and whatever else the data-engineering team had exposed — and each executed call was logged with its target dataset, filters, result cardinality, and source refresh timestamp. The trace renderer walked that log: one step per consequential operation, with the model composing the *sentence* for each step from the logged operation and its actual result. So the skeleton of the trace — what was queried, in what order, from which dataset, refreshed when — could not be invented, because it was read from execution records; the source chips and timestamps came from the log, not the model. The residual risk sits exactly where a security reviewer would look: the natural-language summarization layer. A model paraphrasing a logged result can still mischaracterize it — "no evidence of X" versus "didn't retrieve the dataset containing X" is the failure I worried about most, and it's why the step schema had an explicit not-found type populated from result cardinality rather than model judgment. That mitigation shipped; adversarial testing of the sentence layer did not, during my window.

What was never built while I was there: recomputability as a verified property. The COO-office standard — sampled cited claims reproducing from accessible sources at ≥95% — is the right bar, and we had no such audit. Deep links from source chips to the exact records were spec'd and partially built (system-level links, not row-level everywhere). Immutable log storage was an engineering commitment I reviewed in a design doc, not something I can personally attest to in production. Trace as shipped internally was a query-derived evidence trail with a model-written presentation layer — structurally grounded in query logs, but presentationally unverified. Calling it "a real evidence trail" outright would overclaim by one layer; calling it "a model-written explanation that looks authoritative" would misdescribe the architecture by two. The vocabulary drift across artifacts ("thinking," "derivation," "decision trail," "trace") maps to the project's chronology, and the fact that a careful reader can't tell that from the artifacts alone is a documentation failure I'd fix before anything else.
