# 3. How was the boundary between “abstract unknowns” and tacit tasks established?

**Category:** The work is understandable but not yet convincing  
**Caliber markers:** Contested decisions, consequence, defensibility

**Question:** The research distinguishes abstract unknowns such as discovery, whitespace, and forecast tracking from tacit tasks such as quotation and product configuration. What criteria determined that boundary, how were risk and reversibility assessed, which borderline tasks were debated, and how was the classification used to govern the roadmap rather than only explain it afterward?

**Reason for investigation:** This taxonomy could be a Staff-level decision framework: use AI when the next step is unclear and evidence must be triangulated, but retain structured human control when experience and judgment dominate. The account-team feedback artifact makes the distinction visible. Yet the examples also complicate it. Forecast risk, whitespace, and discovery contain substantial tacit judgment, while quotation may include automatable structured steps. A binary split may hide differences in consequence, confidence, reviewability, and permission to act.

A public-product PM would ask whether the taxonomy predicted user value and failure cost. An enterprise systems leader would ask whether it determined human confirmation, logging, escalation, and autonomy levels. At Staff level, the designer should show a system that made subsequent decisions easier for multiple teams.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would provide decision criteria, examples that moved categories, and evidence that PMs or engineers used the framework when scoping additional features. If the classification was created mainly to narrate why these chosen features made sense, I would treat it as a useful retrospective model rather than force multiplication.

## Answer

The distinction came from a failure pattern, and I'll describe the criteria as they actually operated rather than as a clean framework. Three questions decided placement. First: is the next step unclear? If the user couldn't name what they'd do next without assembling evidence — discovery, whitespace, slip risk — the task went left. Second: where does the time go? If time went to gathering and cross-referencing, AI helped; if it went to judgment applied at every step — quotation, product configuration — AI interaction was slower than the practiced human loop. That second criterion had a measurable proxy I used in walkthroughs: when I asked "what would you do next," experts answered instantly for tacit tasks and haltingly for abstract ones. Third, the enterprise one: consequence and reversibility. A wrong whitespace suggestion wastes attention; a wrong quote configuration creates a contractual artifact.

The borderline debates were real and two are material. Forecast tracking was the contested one — my engineering lead argued it was tacit through and through, because forecast categories are judgment calls an AE defends with their job. He was half right. We resolved it by splitting the task: *evidence assembly* for the forecast call (which deals moved, what changed, what's missing) went to AI; the *category call itself* stayed untouched — the AI was never allowed to propose moving a deal from Commit to Best Case. That split is visible in the shipped Trace examples: they end at "concluded the three named deals as highest slip risk, ranked by evidence-gap severity," never at "downgrade this deal." The second debate was renewal-outcome tagging. The dropdown (Full renewal, Upsell, Downsell, Churn, Displacement) looked automatable from usage data, and a data engineer prototyped a classifier informally. We kept it human, mostly because a wrong churn label propagates into territory planning — a consequence argument, not a capability one.

It served both governance and narrative. Governing: it removed conversational quoting from scope (the decision in Q1), and my lead PM reused the board twice in later scoping — once to reject an "AI next-best-action" pitch from an adjacent team, once to greenlight the renewal-health brief that fed the AI panel's suggestion list. Those are two real instances of other people using the instrument without me in the room, which is the actual extent of its force multiplication. Narrating: the polished two-column artifact with the green check and red stop sign was made months after the decisions, for the case study and for an exec readout. The live version was messier — a FigJam with tasks that moved sides twice.

The binary has no axis for confidence or autonomy level. "AI-appropriate" collapsed everything from "retrieve and summarize" to "rank by severity," and those deserve different human-confirmation regimes. The COO-office review later made essentially this criticism through its controls table, and it's correct. If I rebuilt it, it would be a 2x2 — evidence-boundedness against consequence — with the autonomy ceiling derived from the quadrant, not from my judgment applied case by case. The binary was the version an organization could remember, and I chose memorability over completeness, knowingly.
