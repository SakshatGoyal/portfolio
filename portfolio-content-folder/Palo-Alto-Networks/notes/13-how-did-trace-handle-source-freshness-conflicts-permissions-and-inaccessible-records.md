# 13. How did Trace handle source freshness, conflicts, permissions, and inaccessible records?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Enterprise craft, consequence, provenance

**Question:** The trace cites Salesforce forecast categories, MEDDPICC and activity timelines, notes, stage history, People.ai calls and meeting attendance, Tableau, and other sources. How did it enforce each system's row-level permissions, reconcile conflicting values and refresh times, show inaccessible evidence, handle stale or missing data, and preserve those controls when a result was exported?

**Reason for investigation:** The visible source list improves on generic citations by naming datasets and last-update times. That is a strong design direction. But freshness is not the same as correctness, and showing a source name is not the same as proving access or lineage. A user may have permission to see the AI conclusion but not the underlying call transcript or customer record. Two systems may define stage, owner, or account health differently. The screenshots do not show how conflict, absence, or permission denial deforms the result.

For public products, these are trust states. For an internal enterprise product handling forecast, customer, pricing, and activity data, they are core product behavior. At Staff level, the designer should have worked with security, governance, and data engineering to make them visible and actionable.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would name the permission and lineage model, source-level deep links, conflict rules, stale thresholds, audit logs, and user-facing states. It would include a design rejected because the system could not support the required trust. If these issues were deferred to engineering, I would question the designer's enterprise readiness.

## Answer

This is the area where the design was most ahead of the shipped system, so I'll answer in two registers: the contract I specified, and what the internal release actually enforced.

Freshness was the part that shipped closest to spec, because it was the part users had explicitly taught me to care about — "which dataset, and when was it last updated" is nearly a direct quote from the research. Every source chip renders three things: the system (Salesforce, People.ai), the dataset within it (Forecast categories, MEDDPICC + Activity Timeline, Stage History, Call transcripts, Meeting attendance), and the last-refresh timestamp, all read from the retrieval log rather than typed by the model. The design also specified a staleness threshold per dataset class — call-transcript staleness of a day is fine, forecast-category staleness on a Thursday forecast call is not — with chips flipping to a warning state past threshold. The threshold state was designed and demo'd; the internal release shipped timestamps without the automatic warning, so freshness judgment stayed with the reader. That's a real gap: showing a timestamp outsources the staleness decision to whoever squints at it.

Conflicts: the design rule was show both, never silently reconcile. If two systems disagreed on stage or close date, the trace step surfaced both values with their sources and freshness, and the conclusion had to be phrased against the fresher source explicitly. I got this rule from watching a forecast argument that was, underneath, two dashboards disagreeing about a close date. The shipped behavior handled the common case (same field, two sources, different refresh) and did not handle semantic conflicts — two systems *defining* account health differently — which we papered over by only exposing one health definition to the AI in the first place. Definitional conflict was avoided by data curation, not solved by design, and I'd describe that accurately as the data-engineering team's quiet contribution.

Permissions are the weak flank. The principle in the spec was retrieval under the user's own authorization: the AI queries what the user could query, so a conclusion never launders inaccessible data. Inaccessible-source states existed in the spec — a source chip rendering as present-but-locked, with the trace step declaring evidence exists that the user can't open, rather than omitting it and warping the derivation. What I can attest to: the spec, the review where the governance architect endorsed present-but-locked over silent omission (silent omission produces differently-wrong answers for different roles, which is worse than visible locks). What I can't attest to: row-level enforcement in production across every source, because the prototype ran entirely on synthetic data and production permission plumbing landed after my day-to-day involvement. Export is where I flagged the unsolved problem in writing: the moment a response leaves the panel — screenshot into Slack today, snapshot feature tomorrow — every permission boundary evaporates, and the trace's authority makes the leak worse, because it carries dataset names and timestamps that make it *look* governed. That flagged risk is a design I rejected in the small: an early snapshot concept that baked the full trace into a shareable image; the enhancement request I wrote instead specifies a link-back artifact with viewer-side permission checks (Q14). Deep links from chips to source records: spec'd everywhere, built for Salesforce objects, absent for People.ai artifacts in my window.

If the question is whether these were deferred to engineering: freshness no, conflicts partially, permissions yes in implementation though not in design intent — and the design intent is documented in a spec engineering worked from, which is the strongest evidence I can offer that the deferral was sequenced, not abdicated.
