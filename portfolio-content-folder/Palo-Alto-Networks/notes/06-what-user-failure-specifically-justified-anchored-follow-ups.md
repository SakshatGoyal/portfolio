# 6. What user failure specifically justified Anchored Follow-Ups?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Problem definition, decisions, defensibility

**Question:** Before building explicit row and column anchors, what did users fail to accomplish in ordinary chat or structured UI? How often did they restate context, refer ambiguously to “this deal,” lose the selected object, open another tool, or receive an answer about the wrong record—and what evidence showed that anchoring was more valuable than better prompt suggestions, persistent selection, side-by-side comparison, or a non-conversational drill-down?

**Reason for investigation:** The prototypes make the interaction understandable. A user asks which forecast opportunities may slip, selects the Stripe Treasury XDR Expansion row, sees it animate into the prompt field, and asks what issues remain. The pattern can reduce pronoun ambiguity and preserve analytical continuity. The case, however, begins from a broad disconnect between chat and structured work and does not show a measured failure baseline or the alternative interactions considered.

A public-product PM would ask whether anchoring increases successful task completion and repeat use. An enterprise product leader would care about wrong-object errors, context drift, and the cost of applying a follow-up to the wrong opportunity. At Staff level, the designer must show why this was a product solution rather than a clever interaction looking for a metric.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would show observed failures, their frequency or severity, alternatives, and the test that made anchoring win. It would name at least one use case where anchoring was the wrong model. Without that evidence, I see strong interaction invention but not yet a defensible product decision.

## Answer

The evidence came from watching the pre-anchor version of our own AI panel fail, not from a measured production baseline — The relevant distinction is that up front. Between the first CopilotKit prototype and the anchored design, there was an intermediate build: chat plus structured table responses, no anchoring. I ran six moderated sessions on it with returning participants from the research round, using the slip-risk and whitespace scenarios. My tally sheet from those sessions is the closest thing to a baseline that exists: across six users I counted fourteen instances of context restatement (retyping an opportunity name the model had just displayed), five ambiguous references ("what about the second one," "this deal") of which three got answers about the wrong record, and four abandonments where the user stopped the thread and went back to the table to re-find their object. The wrong-record answers were the radicalizing ones — the model answered confidently about a neighboring row, and one participant didn't notice until I asked. In an enterprise sales tool that's not friction, that's a false risk assessment with a straight path into a forecast call.

The alternatives were on the table and each had a real champion. Better prompt suggestions: cheapest, and we did it anyway, but suggestions help you start, not continue — the failures were mid-thread. Implicit context carryover (the model just remembers "the second one"): my senior front-end engineer favored it as zero-UI; I killed it on inspectability grounds — when implicit context is wrong, the user has no artifact to check or remove, which is precisely how the wrong-record failure becomes invisible. Persistent selection in the table driving a side panel — a non-conversational drill-down: the strongest rival, and the better design for single-object depth; it lost because it couldn't express the branching move (opportunity → platform column → sales plays) that the walkthroughs showed was where cross-tool pain actually lived. Side-by-side comparison: deferred, still not shipped, still the most-requested absence.

Anchoring won because it made context a visible, removable object: select the generated row, watch it ease into the prompt field as a chip carrying the object's identity and key fields, ask the next thing. In the follow-up sessions with five users on the anchored build, restatement effectively disappeared and — the part I cared about — wrong-object answers went to zero for anchored steps, because the object ID travels with the prompt rather than being inferred. Those are small-n moderated observations, not metrics; the production-scale signal came later through arc telemetry, which shows continuation, not failure-prevention, and I've kept those claims separate deliberately.

Where anchoring was the wrong model: broad discovery questions ("which of my accounts is weakest on Cortex?") need no anchor and got none — forcing one would be ceremony; and true multi-object comparison defeats a single-chip model entirely. One participant tried to anchor two rows to compare them, couldn't, and worked around it with two threads — that's a design failure I logged, proposed a two-anchor compare mode for, and never shipped. The defensible claim is: anchoring solved observed mid-thread context loss and wrong-object answers for single-object chains, was validated qualitatively before build, and the measured production story is adoption-shaped, not failure-rate-shaped, because we never instrumented the failure the way we instrumented the success.
