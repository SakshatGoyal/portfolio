# 2. What research supports the claims about spreadsheet-native users and mixed AI sentiment?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Provenance, defensibility, narrative command

**Question:** How many account-team members across Americas, EMEA, and APJC participated, which roles and segments did they represent, what research methods were used, and what evidence showed that structured, tactile analysis was a learned professional fluency rather than simple resistance to change? What important regional, role, or tenure differences did you find?

**Reason for investigation:** The case says the designer spoke with account teams across three major regions and found mixed opinions. The visual research artifacts add texture, and the workshop synthesis records concerns that AI “insights” lacked depth, long chats lost focus, summaries flattened tensions, and teams tried to automate judgment-heavy work. But the participant count, protocol, recruitment, raw quotes, and dissent are absent. The label “spreadsheet-native” is memorable, yet it risks becoming a broad persona that makes the eventual design feel inevitable.

A public-product PM would want evidence that the interaction pattern serves a real segment with a recurring job. An enterprise leader would expect differences among AEs, sales engineers, managers, operations, and specialists. At Staff level, a global claim should survive questions about representation and conflicting needs.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want the sample, role mix, methods, contradictory findings, and at least one design decision that changed because of research. A strong answer would explain for whom chat was actually useful and where the structured model was less effective. If the story relies on a few vocal spreadsheet users, I would discount the global framing and question whether the design overfit expert behavior.

## Answer

The number I can stand behind is nineteen people across the three theaters, spread over about six weeks in mid-2025: nine account executives, four sales engineers, three first-line sales managers, two sales operations analysts, and one renewals specialist. Americas was over-represented (ten of the nineteen); EMEA gave me six, mostly through a hybrid session hosted from the London office with about fifteen people in the room and on the call — that session is the one recorded artifact I kept; APJC gave me three, all remote, all AEs. Method was unglamorous: screen-share walkthroughs of their actual Monday routine, me asking them to narrate the tools as they touched them, plus that one group session which was closer to a structured complaint hour than a workshop. No survey, no statistical claim. When the case study says "mixed opinions," the underlying texture is roughly: a third curious, a third indifferent, a third actively hostile after having been burned by an earlier "insights" rollout that summarized things they already knew.

"Spreadsheet-native" came out of the walkthroughs, not the group session. What I actually watched was people running the same reconciliation across a Salesforce list view, a forecast grid, and a personal spreadsheet — cross-referencing by eye, keyboard-first, fast. The claim that this was learned professional fluency rather than change resistance rests on a specific observation: the same people who dismissed the chatbot were enthusiastic about *new* structured tools. The renewals specialist had built her own Airtable. These weren't laggards; they were experts whose expertise was encoded in tabular manipulation. I sketched this as the caricature that made it into the case — overlapping grids with hop-arrows — because that was genuinely what their screens looked like.

The differences mattered, and the persona flattens them. Sales engineers were the most AI-receptive role — their work is closer to research synthesis, and two of the four asked for exactly the cross-source summarization we later built. Managers cared about defensibility above all; the line "a platform name doesn't pass as a source" is nearly verbatim from an EMEA district manager in the London session. The APJC sample was too thin to support any regional claim, and I never made one internally, though the case's "across Americas, EMEA, and APJC" framing implies more balance than existed. The contradictory finding important: the two ops analysts *wanted* long-form narrative summaries — for QBR prep, flattening was the point, not the failure. Chat was actually fine for them. The design decision that changed because of research: my first AI-panel concept opened with a conversational empty state; after the walkthroughs I rebuilt it around suggestion chips tied to structured outputs and made the table, not the paragraph, the default response body. Research cost me a design I liked.

The provenance limits are straightforward: I have no consent-logged repository of transcripts, recruitment was through the sales-ops partner's network (so it skewed toward people already engaged with tooling), and nineteen is nineteen. The workshop synthesis artifacts — the insights-versus-recommendations gap sketch, the abstract/tacit board — are my syntheses of those sessions, reviewed by my lead PM, not independently validated. If someone claims the design overfit expert behavior: partially true. We optimized for the tenured majority and accepted that a first-year AE, who has no spreadsheet fluency yet, might have been better served by the conversational form we deprioritized. That trade was discussed once, explicitly, and we chose the experts because they carried the forecast.
