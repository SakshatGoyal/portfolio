# 14. What evidence supports Trace adoption, and how were snapshots kept defensible after sharing?

**Category:** Missing evidence and incomplete product reasoning  
**Caliber markers:** Consequence, craft, enterprise judgment

**Question:** The case says people snapped a response and its trace into Slack threads and that the behavior became frequent enough to prompt a snapshot feature request. How many users and sessions did this involve, what is the actual snapshot rate, what did recipients do next, and how would the exported artifact preserve generation time, source refresh, owner, permissions, expiry, and a link back to current data across Slack, Salesforce notes, and email?

**Reason for investigation:** The snapshot behavior is potentially Trace's most meaningful adoption signal: a user is willing to carry the result into another team's decision workflow. Yet the impact card leaves Snapshot Rate blank, while the metrics note only proposes a 15% target. A feature request is not adoption. Static evidence can also outlive its source, circulate outside the original permission boundary, and continue influencing decisions after the data changes.

A public-product PM would distinguish a request, a shipped enhancement, repeated use, and downstream action. An enterprise leader would treat snapshot staleness and permission leakage as serious operational risks. At Staff level, the designer should define the lifecycle of shared evidence, not only make capture convenient.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want observed counts, participant context, the resulting product requirement, recipient behavior, and the governance model for shared artifacts. A strong answer would show that snapshotting was instrumented and linked back to a live, permission-checked view. If the evidence is anecdotal, the story should say so and avoid presenting Trace adoption as an outcome.

## Answer

The evidence is observational and I kept it deliberately unlaundered: the Snapshot Rate card in the case PDF is blank because the feature didn't exist and the behavior it would measure was never instrumented. What existed was people improvising the feature with screenshots. My count, which I did by hand: across two forecast-prep Slack channels I had access to, twenty-seven posts over four weeks contained a screenshot of an AI-panel response with its trace expanded — I tallied them because the pattern kept appearing in threads I was watching for feedback, not because we had planned to measure it. Maybe a dozen distinct posters, concentrated around forecast-call cadence (Mondays and end-of-quarter weeks), and the modal usage was defending a number: the response's conclusion plus the trace pasted under a challenged forecast position. I could see recipients react and occasionally reply "ok, moving on" — but I could not systematically observe what recipients *did*, and I cannot claim downstream action beyond the thread behavior itself. Twenty-seven screenshots from a dozen people is a behavioral signal strong enough to promote a feature request; it is not adoption, and the case study's phrasing ("frequent enough that snapshotting was promoted to an enhancement") is the accurate strength of claim while the blank card sits there as the admission that nothing stronger exists.

The enhancement request is where the governance thinking lives, because the improvised behavior was actively dangerous in exactly the ways an enterprise reviewer would list. A screenshot of a trace is stale evidence wearing an audit trail's clothes: it carries dataset names and refresh timestamps that make it look governed while being a dead image that outlives the data, escapes every permission boundary, and keeps circulating. So the spec I wrote defined a snapshot as a live artifact, not an image: generation timestamp, per-source refresh timestamps frozen as displayed at generation, the owner identity, and — the load-bearing part — a link back to a permission-checked live view that re-renders the same query against current data and tells the viewer whether the conclusion still holds. Viewer-side permission checks meant a snapshot shared into a channel would show its conclusion and trace skeleton but lock source detail the viewer couldn't access directly. I also specified an expiry label after which the artifact nags rather than asserts. The contested decision inside that spec: my lead PM wanted plain image export shipped fast because users were doing it anyway — the argument being that a sanctioned screenshot beats an unsanctioned one. I argued shipping the image version would freeze the dangerous behavior in place and make the governed version look like a downgrade later. We shipped neither in my window; the request entered the backlog with the governed design attached, and the documented status at my exit was: behavior observed, requirement written, lifecycle designed, nothing built, nothing measured. The 15% snapshot-rate target in the metrics doc is a proposed threshold for a feature that would need to exist first.

What I'd concede to a skeptic: the twenty-seven count has obvious selection bias (channels I could see, weeks I was looking), the improvised screenshots mean Trace's most-cited adoption story is also its clearest governance failure, and if the governed snapshot shipped and the rate came in under target, the right reading would be that defensibility mattered to a vocal minority in forecast workflows — which is still the population that carries the number-defending burden, and still, in my view, the population Trace was for.
