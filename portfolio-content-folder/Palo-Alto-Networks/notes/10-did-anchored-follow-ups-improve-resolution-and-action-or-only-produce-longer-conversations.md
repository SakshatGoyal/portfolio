# 10. Did Anchored Follow-Ups improve resolution and action, or only produce longer conversations?

**Category:** The visible outcome is unconvincing  
**Caliber markers:** Consequence with a causal chain, product judgment

**Question:** How did the team determine whether higher arc rate and depth meant a user reached a correct, verified answer faster, rather than needed more steps to recover from ambiguity? What happened to abandonment, correction, time-to-resolution, re-navigation, downstream CRM updates, manager decisions, or customer actions?

**Reason for investigation:** The interaction mechanism is plausible: preserving object context should reduce restatement and make a deal → issue → evidence chain easier. But engagement is not the intended operating value. The available material has no abandonment result, resolution definition, elapsed time, correction rate, or downstream action. The COO-office analysis explicitly notes that the same 4.3-step arc could represent useful exploration or unresolved wandering.

A public-product PM would refuse to use depth as a north-star metric without a resolution guardrail. An enterprise product leader would measure the entire team's time, since a seller's faster answer may create additional verification work for Sales Ops or a manager. At Staff level, the designer should connect the UI decision to a falsifiable behavior and business mechanism.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want the resolution metric, guardrails, observed or proposed comparison design, and one example of a real decision completed with less rework. If the designer can only show deeper engagement and enthusiasm, I would treat the metrics as adoption signals, not impact.

## Answer

The premise deserves a straight answer: we demonstrated deeper engagement, and we did not demonstrate resolution. Abandonment — three-plus steps without resolution, the metric I designated as the principal guardrail in the definitions doc — was never instrumented during my window, because it requires a resolution event we hadn't designed. That is the single largest gap between what the feature promised operationally and what the measurement system could see, and it's a design debt, not an oversight: I defined the guardrail knowing we couldn't yet compute it, on the theory that naming it would force the instrumentation. It forced a proposal, not a pipeline.

What we had instead were weaker but real signals, and I'll give them at their actual strength. First, the resolution proxies in the interaction design: a typical response ended with a terse conclusion, an in-scope/out-of-scope statement, and a suggested next step, so "did the user take a suggested next step or export" was observable in principle — snapshot-intent clicks and thread-end-after-conclusion patterns leaned encouraging in the five-week window, but I never formalized either into a resolution definition, so I won't dress them as one. Second, the Slack behavior: people carrying a response and its trace into forecast-prep channels to defend a number is an action, not engagement — a person doesn't paste evidence into a thread their manager reads unless the analysis concluded something they were prepared to stand behind. My informal count (Q14 has the details) was the closest thing to an action metric we had. Third, one concrete workflow anecdote I verified myself: a district manager ran Monday slip-risk prep from the panel — forecast question, anchored deep-dive on the flagged deal, trace expanded, screenshot into the forecast-call thread — and told my lead PM it replaced her prior circuit through the forecast grid, activity timeline, and call-transcript search. One user, unmeasured elapsed time, genuinely changed workflow. That is the entire verified causal chain, and I present it as an existence proof, not a result.

Could the 4.3-step depth be recovery-from-ambiguity rather than productive exploration? Partially, yes, and the strongest evidence that some of it was comes from our own failure reports: early expansion-type steps occasionally anchored the wrong object class (a product column when the user meant the product's opportunities), and the user's next two steps were repair. Sessions like that inflate depth while representing friction. The counter-evidence is the concentration pattern — depth clustered in the two scenarios where users had told us the cross-tool assembly cost lived, rather than uniformly across all query types, which is what pure wandering would look like — but distinguishing the two definitively needs the correction-rate and re-navigation instrumentation the COO-office plan later specified, and I endorse essentially all of that plan's Phase 1.

Under this uncertainty, we did not use depth as a success metric internally for prioritization — the usage spike plus the Slack behavior, not the depth number, is what justified accelerating data-engineering work to expose more sources. The decision I'd criticize: shipping the case-study impact section with engagement numbers foregrounded and no abandonment line, which reads as impact where it should read as adoption. A public-product PM refusing depth as a north star without a resolution guardrail is applying exactly the standard I wrote into the definitions doc and then failed to hold the narrative to.
