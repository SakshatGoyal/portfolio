# 9. Can you defend the 23% arc rate and 4.3-step arc depth without the case's benchmark errors?

**Category:** Missing evidence and an existing credibility concern  
**Caliber markers:** Consequence, provenance, defensibility

**Question:** What were the sample size, observation window, exposed-user population, eligible-session rules, product version, comparison cohort, and confidence interval for the reported 23% arc rate and 4.3-step arc depth? What is the named source and like-for-like definition for the 11%–15% reference, and why does the polished case label a 4.3-step depth as “+12pts over industry standard” when depth is not a percentage?

**Reason for investigation:** These are the headline metrics, and the supporting metrics note correctly warns that they lack the context needed to establish operational value. It also states that arc rate is two points below the proposed 25% target, that depth is about 2.2–2.3 steps above a 2.0–2.1 reference, and that the case's “+12pts” label is dimensionally wrong. The reference comes from user-supplied enterprise-assistant assumptions rather than a named, inspectable study, and baseline “multi-turn” may not equal object-anchored continuation.

A public-product PM will challenge the denominator, exposure, novelty effect, and target. An enterprise leader will ask whether long arcs were productive or reflected unresolved wandering. At Staff level, a metric-unit error in a polished impact section is not a cosmetic problem; it undermines trust in the designer's analytical judgment.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would correct the artifact, provide the measurement details, separate internal baseline from external reference, and state what cannot be concluded. Owning the mistake would restore credibility. Defending the “twice industry standard” framing without comparable data would materially weaken my confidence in the story.

## Answer

Let me correct the artifact first, because the "+12pts" label under Arc Depth is my error and it's the kind that costs trust. Arc depth is measured in steps; the defensible statement is roughly +2.2 steps against a 2.0–2.1-step reference, not "+12pts over industry standard." What happened mechanically: the impact cards in the case PDF were built as duplicated components, the caption belonged to the arc-rate card (where +8 to +12 percentage points against the 11–15% reference is at least dimensionally coherent), and I never corrected the copy on the second card. The metrics-definitions note I wrote separately gets it right, which makes the polished version worse, not better — the place most people look is the place I was least careful. I've owned that ordering.

Now the measurement, stated with its actual epistemics. The 23% arc rate and 4.3-step arc depth came from our own telemetry over roughly a five-week window following the internal release, across on the order of 140 enabled internal users — account-team members and adjacent roles who had the AI panel turned on. Eligible sessions were sittings with at least one AI-panel query; a session with two or more object-anchored steps counted as an arc; depth averaged steps across arcs only, excluding one-and-dones, per the definitions doc. What the measurement does *not* have, and I said so when I reported it internally: no control group, no cohort stratification, no confidence interval, and no correction for novelty — a five-week window on a newly launched feature with an enthusiastic early population almost certainly flatters both numbers. There is also a denominator subtlety that cuts the other way: unanchored-but-related follow-ups don't count as arc steps, so the arc rate undercounts true continuation. I can't tell you the net direction of those two biases, and neither can anyone else without the controlled comparison.

On the reference range: the 11–15% multi-turn rate and 2.0–2.1 depth are planning references I supplied from general enterprise-assistant knowledge when we set targets pre-launch — they are not a named study, not a PANW baseline, and not like-for-like, since generic "multi-turn" counts related questions while our arc definition requires object-anchored steps, a stricter bar. Those numbers were useful only as planning placeholders, as the metrics note states, and our own telemetry later replaced them. The "over twice industry standard" framing in the case study is the marketing-shaped version of "4.3 against a 2.1 planning reference," and I'd retract the word "industry" entirely if I rewrote it — there is no inspectable industry study behind it, and defending it as a benchmark would be indefensible.

What survives after all the discounting, in my view: the internal target we set before launch was 25% arc rate, and we came in at 23% — two points *below* our own bar, which the case study conspicuously doesn't mention and I will. Depth at 4.3 cleared its 3.0 target by enough margin that even a generous novelty discount likely leaves it above. And the shape of the data was the genuinely informative part: continuation wasn't uniform — it concentrated in the forecast/slip-risk and whitespace scenarios, which matched the abstract-unknowns thesis. What cannot be concluded: that arcs were productive (no resolution or abandonment instrumentation), that the features caused the engagement (no control), or that any of it transfers past the early-adopter window. The numbers earned a controlled trial — the COO-office review reached the same posture independently — and nothing stronger.
