# 12. Which scope recommendation did the product team accept, reject, or defer—and why?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Consequence, decisions, force multiplication

**Question:** The deliverable recommends several possible cuts—Technology and Human Tension, Coordinated Builders, a T-shaped combination, or individual problems such as Craftsmanship versus Brand Perception or Proven Models versus Disruptive Pricing. What criteria ranked these options, which direction did leadership choose, and what product work changed as a result?

**Reason for investigation:** The scope matrix is where the research should cash out as product judgment. It clearly warns that each problem is massive and that a T-shaped approach could explode scope. Yet the story presents multiple recommendations without a final decision, decision-maker, feasibility comparison, or explanation of why one opportunity creates more value than another. “The next step for the product team” remains future tense.

A public-product PM would expect an opportunity scorecard grounded in user severity, frequency, differentiation, feasibility, risk, and strategic fit. An enterprise leader would also consider data access, model reliability, security, integration, governance, and organizational readiness. At Staff level, producing a rich option space is incomplete unless the designer helps the organization make a hard choice.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want the selection criteria, stakeholders, contested tradeoff, chosen or stopped direction, and the designer’s recommendation even if leadership disagreed. A strong answer would show how the architecture reduced the option space and changed an MVP brief or research investment. If no choice followed, the work should be framed as an exploratory map awaiting product commitment rather than a foundation that demonstrably guided building.

## Answer

None was accepted in my presence, and I won't invent a decision the record doesn't hold. Here is exactly what happened, what I argued, and where my influence demonstrably stopped.

The scope matrix was built to end the engagement with a choosing meeting. Four ways in, each with a recommendation: Approach 1, pick a theme across all four archetypes — recommended Technology and Human Tension; Approach 2, pick an archetype across its four tensions — recommended Coordinated Builders; Approach 3, the T-shape combining both — flagged with an explicit caution that each problem statement is already a massive design scope and the T risks exploding it; Approach 4, pick a single problem — recommended Craftsmanship vs. Brand Perception or Proven Models vs. Disruptive Pricing. The ranking criteria were stated in the room even though no scorecard shipped: evidence density (how many modules and which interviews sat behind each cell), differentiation from general-purpose assistants (the "boring areas" steer, operationalized — cells where incumbent AI tools have no story), and buildable surface area (whether a first product slice could demonstrate value without solving an entire archetype). Interview confirmation weighted Technology and Human Tension up; scope containment weighted Approach 4 up.

My own recommendation, stated as mine: enter through Approach 4 — a single cell, specifically Craftsmanship vs. Brand Perception — while holding Approach 2's Coordinated Builders as the expansion path, so the single cell wasn't a dead end but the first move of a column strategy. I argued it in the final presentation: the matrix itself says every problem statement is a massive scope, so the only entry that forces a falsifiable MVP is one cell; a theme-wide entry (Approach 1) would seduce the team into building a general framework again — and the one thing this engagement proved is how much gravity pulls toward frameworks over products. The PM pushed back in exactly the direction I'd have pushed in their seat: a single cell risks building something too narrow to demonstrate institutional value; the Head of AI was drawn to Approach 1's theme because it mapped cleanly onto the Institute's public AI-and-organizations research agenda. That tension — demonstrable focus versus institutional legibility — was the real contested tradeoff in the room, and it did not resolve in the room.

What the record shows after that is a deferral, and I'll state it plainly because the future tense in the shipped document is the evidence: "the next step for the product team will involve exploring different combinations from the table to define the scope for an MVP." The decision was explicitly moved past the engagement boundary — into the team's own planning cycle, on their calendar, without me. My contract concluded with the delivery; the follow-on scope-selection session I proposed facilitating was acknowledged and never scheduled, which is the org-friction texture of working as an external researcher for a research institute: the engagement's end date was harder than the decision's due date. No leadership decision, no MVP brief, no chosen approach ever came back to me, and the folder contains none.

So the accountable framing of this project's consequence is: I reduced an unanswerable field to four ranked options with a named recommendation and a warning label on the seductive-but-dangerous one, and delivered it to the people entitled to choose — and the causal chain ends there, at "delivered with recommendations," future tense preserved. If leadership later chose a direction, the choice happened outside my visibility and I will not claim credit for it; if they chose nothing, then the work's precise classification is an exploratory map awaiting product commitment, exactly as your question frames it — with one important addendum: the map was delivered *decision-shaped*, and the difference between a landscape and a ranked option set with entry criteria is the difference I was hired to make. Whether the organization crossed the line I walked them to is corroboration work — a question for the Head of AI, not for my portfolio.
