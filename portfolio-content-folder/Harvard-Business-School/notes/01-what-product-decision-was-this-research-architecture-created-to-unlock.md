# 1. What product decision was this research architecture created to unlock?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Problem altitude, consequence, narrative command

**Question:** What specific decision was the HBS AI team unable to make before this work—whether to build an executive AI product, which executive situation to serve, what strategic job to support, or how AI should behave—and what deadline, investment, or organizational consequence made that decision important?

**Reason for investigation:** The story begins at impressive altitude: “executives” and “strategy” were too broad, and the team wanted AI to support strategic thinking rather than production work. The designer says the work gave product teams “a foundation to build on before building.” What remains missing is the actual decision context. I do not know what product concept, roadmap, budget, research program, or leadership commitment was waiting on the answer. Without that anchor, a very large research system can read as an exploration of an intellectually rich space rather than a response to a consequential product uncertainty.

A public-product PM needs to know what decision the research de-risked and what opportunity cost existed. An enterprise research leader needs to know who commissioned the work, what evidence threshold they required, and when the research would be considered sufficient. At Staff level, ambiguity is not itself proof of altitude; the designer must convert ambiguity into a decision an organization can make.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would name the sponsor by role, the product or program at stake, the decision deadline, the options under consideration, and the evidence threshold for continuing, narrowing, or stopping. It would also explain why a smaller discovery effort would have been inadequate. If the architecture led to a clear investment or scope decision, the breadth becomes strategic. If the assignment was simply to explore executive AI broadly, the work may demonstrate sophisticated research craft without yet proving Staff-level product consequence.

## Answer

There was no product waiting on us. The Digital Data Design Institute is a research institute, not a product company, and the brief I received — from the Institute's Head of AI — was one sentence: "we need to understand strategy among executives." No product concept, no division, no budget line I ever saw. So the first thing I did as the owner of the research was manufacture the decision context the brief lacked, because research without a decision at the end of it is just reading.

The decision I built the architecture to unlock, and the one I kept repeating in every check-in, was: **which executive situation deserves the Institute's first product bet in strategic AI, and what would that product have to do differently from a general assistant.** The sponsor mechanics were real even if informal. The Head of AI had a standing question from institute leadership about what the AI group should propose building beyond its existing research tools; the Product Manager who joined our check-ins needed a defensible scope brief before their team could commit a build cycle in the fall. Neither of them ever gave me a signed evidence threshold. What they gave me, verbally, across roughly biweekly check-ins from March onward, was a working test I wrote down after the second one: "enough that we could pick one use case and defend the pick to people who weren't in the room." That became my internal bar — not proof, defensibility of a choice.

The deadline structure was ours more than theirs. We set a mid-April checkpoint for having the synthesis (archetypes) presentable, and a May delivery for the full package. The mid-April date was real enough that when the clustering collapsed on April 10 I was, in my own words in the working chats, "way past our deadline" — that deadline was a check-in with the Head of AI and the PM, not an arbitrary calendar mark. The final 45-slide presentation and the six research documents landed in May, and the scope matrix in `02-defining-scope.md` is the decision instrument itself: four explicit ways to cut into the problem space, each with a recommendation, so that the meeting after mine could be a choosing meeting rather than another understanding meeting.

The nominally aligned alternative was to treat the engagement as an open-ended literature landscape for a research institute, delivered as a beautifully organized reading of the field. That would have been easier, faster, and arguably more aligned with what a research institute usually consumes. I rejected it because both of my actual counterparts were product people in behavior if not in title: every question the PM asked in check-ins was a scoping question ("if we only built for one of these, which one"), and a landscape doesn't answer scoping questions. The cost of my choice was real: forcing everything toward a decision meant building routing, classification, and scope machinery that tripled the work.

I cannot name a dollar figure, a board date, or a formal investment gate that my work fed, because none was ever disclosed to me and I'm not certain a formal one existed at the time. The opportunity cost was the sponsor's attention and roughly four months of a funded external engagement, not a committed roadmap. What I can defend is that I converted a brief with no decision in it into a package whose last artifact is literally a recommendation table — and that "the next step for the product team" in that document is written in future tense because, at handoff, the choice was genuinely still theirs to make. Whether they made it is Question 12 and 16's problem, and I address that directly there.
