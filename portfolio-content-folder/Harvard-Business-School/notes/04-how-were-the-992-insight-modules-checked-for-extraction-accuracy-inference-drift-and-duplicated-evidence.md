# 4. How were the 992 insight modules checked for extraction accuracy, inference drift, and duplicated evidence?

**Category:** The work is understandable but does not yet go far enough  
**Caliber markers:** Decisions, provenance, defensibility

**Question:** What exactly qualified as one insight module, how were papers segmented, how did you prevent one source from becoming many apparently independent findings, and what human review measured whether empirical, inferred, and speculative labels were assigned correctly?

**Reason for investigation:** The structured module is one of the strongest artifacts in the project. It captures an insight statement, strategic dilemma, underlying beliefs, expected versus actual outcomes, broader implications, supporting context, and evidence labels. That is a thoughtful attempt to preserve latent meaning while making material computable. The risk is that extraction creates false precision: one paper can yield many modules, model-generated implications can drift beyond the source, and repeated claims can appear as corroboration merely because they were decomposed multiple times.

The model-evaluation mini case also shows that nearly identical final scores concealed materially different criterion-level reasoning. That makes module-level quality more important than the aggregate pipeline count. A public-product PM needs confidence that prioritization is not being driven by artifacts of segmentation. An enterprise AI leader needs sampling plans, reviewer agreement, exception handling, and traceability.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want the module schema, segmentation rule, duplicate-detection method, validation sample, reviewer roles, disagreement rate, and examples of modules corrected or removed. A strong answer would distinguish source evidence from model interpretation at every layer and show how downstream clusters were adjusted for source concentration. If the modules were mainly spot-checked informally, I would treat the architecture as an inventive research prototype rather than a reliable evidence system.

## Answer

Let me first reconcile the number, because if I can't do that credibly nothing else in this answer matters. 992 is the manufactured count — modules produced by the extraction prompts across all cells. "Around 950" is what I told the categorization framework in late March, after obvious malformations were dropped. "Around 900" appears in retrospectives with post-INVALID shrinkage. And the tagging CSVs carry much smaller numbers — 222 modules scored on the industry axes, 259 rows in the clustering matrix — because those passes ran on the categorical-module subset: modules that read as decision narratives, not opinion or synthesis fragments. The counts drift because "module" was recounted at different pipeline stages with different survival rules, and I never froze a canonical manifest. An enterprise research lead would call that a version-control failure of the evidence base itself. Correct. It's the single cheapest thing I'd fix in a rerun.

What a module was: a three-part unit — Insight Statement; Executive Decision-Making Context (strategic dilemma, underlying beliefs and biases, expected versus actual outcomes, broader implications); Supporting Context — with every claim tagged [Empirical], [Inferred], or [Speculative]. Segmentation followed decision narratives, not page counts: one paper yielded as many modules as it contained distinct executive decisions, which captures richness but creates an independence problem. Extraction ran staged: Prompt 1A extracted in an investigative-professor persona, 1B stress-tested as a devil's advocate, 1C filtered for relevance and citation. The definitional layer underneath was mine and I enforced it hard: an insight is a juxtaposition of two facts, not a summary — "CitiBank reduced branches to focus on digital" is knowledge; the insight is that executives may have overestimated digital adoption. Weak insight states the end outcome; strong insight reveals the underlying cause.

Validation was informal spot-checking plus structural controls, not measured human review. There was no sampling plan, no second rater, no disagreement rate, because there was no second human in the loop. What existed instead: the two-run consistency protocol (same modules, same prompt, fresh chats — does the tagging reproduce?), the INVALID gate that discarded modules scoring zero across all twenty-one routing questions, structure-inconsistency flags that forced scoring rather than skipping, and my own reading during synthesis, where the manual archetype pass meant I re-read modules against their source papers. That last layer caught the incident that best answers your duplicated-evidence concern: during archetype drafting in April, the model had hung an entire archetype's evidence off a single module — MODULE 2 – C4-I2 cited for everything — which I flagged in writing as both an accuracy failure and a plagiarism risk, and forced a re-spread across the available modules. That catch is reassuring and damning simultaneously: reassuring because the review layer worked when it fired; damning because it fired by luck of my attention, not by systematic duplicate detection. There was no fuzzy-matching pass to find the same McKinsey claim decomposed into three apparently independent modules across cells. Source-concentration adjustment happened only where I noticed it.

The alternative I turned down: a formal validation sample — pull fifty modules, verify every [Empirical] tag against its source page, measure my own error rate. Two evenings of work, and I didn't do it, partly deadline pressure and partly, because the pipeline's momentum made auditing feel like slowing down — exactly the trap the engineer's "you've built a pipeline" comment named. So the verdict you offered in the question is the one I'd sign: this was an inventive research prototype with unusually good structural hygiene — evidence tags, staged extraction, kill gates — and without the measured human adjudication that would make it a reliable evidence system. The downstream protection was that the deliverable's problem statements cite modules and the flimsier success criteria carry [speculated] flags; the blending risk lives in the middle tier of [Inferred] content, where model interpretation and source evidence are separated by a tag I applied by trust and checked by sampling my own attention.
