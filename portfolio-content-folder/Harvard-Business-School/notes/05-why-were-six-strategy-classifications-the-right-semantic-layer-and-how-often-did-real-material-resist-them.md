# 5. Why were six strategy classifications the right semantic layer, and how often did real material resist them?

**Category:** The work is understandable but does not yet go far enough  
**Caliber markers:** Decisions, craft, defensibility

**Question:** How did you derive corporate, business, functional and tactical, crisis and adaptive, innovation and growth, and personal leadership as the six strategy types? What alternatives were considered, what were the tie-breaker rules, and what happened when a module genuinely belonged to several levels?

**Reason for investigation:** Sorting each module by decision type is a smart response to a real synthesis problem: executive behavior and AI opportunity can blur when evidence from different strategic contexts is combined. The classification example shows a scored comparison and a single winning category. What is not visible is why the categories are collectively exhaustive, whether they are mutually exclusive, or whether a forced winner discarded useful cross-level relationships.

The work later argues that strategic decisions are layered and interdependent, which sits uneasily beside a single-label classification system. A public-product PM would ask whether the taxonomy led to materially different product requirements. An enterprise leader would ask about ontology ownership, versioning, edge cases, and whether teams could apply the categories consistently without the designer.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would show the theoretical sources, competing taxonomies, confusion matrix or reviewer disagreement, multi-label cases, and a decision changed by this layer. I also want an example the system refused or escalated. If the six classes made the dataset easier to navigate but did not alter product choices, they are useful information architecture. If they became a shared, tested language that guided multiple teams, they begin to demonstrate Staff-level multiplication.

## Answer

The derivation is on record and I'll compress it: academic literature gives you three stable strategy levels — corporate, business, functional. I kept those and added three the literature under-serves: crisis/adaptive (risk work is prevalent in practice and weakly theorized as strategy), innovation/growth (barely a "subject" in academia because there's no how-to manual for innovation), and personal leadership (peripheral references, no solid course material). The ambition was MECE. The reality — and this is the part your question is really probing — is that MECE was the design goal of the classification, not an achieved property of the material, and I knew it within the first tagging week.

The mechanics of how a module got classified matter here, because I didn't trust a naked label request. The strategy pass ran against a Strategy Classification Handbook with type descriptions and examples, an alignment guideline, tie-breaker rules, and explicit guardrails. Each module was scored across five lenses — decision layer, strategic tension, strategic intent, scope and horizon, cognitive framing — and the classification followed the totals. The worked example I kept in the deck shows the machinery directly: a supply-chain module scoring 20 on functional/tactical against 15 business-level and 10 corporate, landing as functional and tactical strategy. Before I trusted any of it, I ran the taxonomy through an adversarial pass — I had the model interrogate the handbook as a sharp undergraduate, twenty-five to thirty questions, challenging distinctions and edge cases — which forced me to sharpen the crisis-versus-corporate boundary (a divestiture under duress is crisis strategy only if the time pressure, not the portfolio logic, dominates the decision) before production tagging began.

How often did material resist? Constantly, and the tell is architectural rather than statistical, because I never built the confusion matrix your question rightly asks for. The five-lens scoring exists precisely because single-cue classification kept splitting: a fintech-integration module is corporate by intent, functional by activity, innovation by framing. The scores forced a winner. What I lost by forcing winners is the thing my own deliverable later asserts — that strategic decisions are layered and interdependent — and The relevant detail is that tension instead of smoothing it: the classification layer treats levels as exclusive to make a corpus navigable; the synthesis layer treats them as interpenetrating because that's what the evidence shows. Those are different tools for different altitudes, but a reader who notices the contradiction is noticing something real. The multi-label alternative was live and I rejected it deliberately: with multi-label tagging, every downstream aggregate (the strategy-type module files that fed clustering) doubles and triples its membership, and cross-contamination — one module's evidence appearing under three strategies and reading as corroboration — was the exact failure I was trying to engineer out.

Did the six classes change a product decision, or just organize the library? Mostly the latter, and I'll say so. Their genuine downstream consequence was distributional: sorting revealed where the corpus was rich (business, functional) and thin (crisis, personal leadership), which shaped both later sourcing and the eventual observation that whole strategy types were underserved terrain for AI support. Personal leadership, my most speculative added category, earned its place late: the ethics-and-judgment material that fed the Principled Orchestrators archetype and the Self-Reflective versus External-Facing principle came disproportionately from modules that only that category had a home for. But no team applied this taxonomy without me, there's no versioned ontology with an owner, and the tie-breaker rules lived in prompt files rather than governance docs. Useful information architecture, tested by adversarial questioning, applied consistently by one operator. Claiming more would be inflation.
