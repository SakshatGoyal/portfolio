# 7. Why did the activity model shift between six situational scales and four clustering axes?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Decisions, provenance, narrative command

**Question:** The portfolio narrative names regulation, modularity, timing, value horizon, knowledge transfer, and market spread as situational scales, while the initial archetype material describes four axes: regulatory exposure, value timeframe, process modularity, and time dependency. Which model came first, why were dimensions removed or combined, and what evidence justified the final choice?

**Reason for investigation:** The situational-axis move appears to be the story’s central original decision. It avoided treating industries as homogeneous and instead compared activities by conditions of work. That is promising. Yet the evidence presents two versions without explaining the transition. Knowledge transfer and market spread could materially distinguish strategic situations; removing them may have simplified the map while narrowing what the system could perceive.

At Staff level, iteration is a strength only when the story shows what failed, what changed, and what was sacrificed. A public-product PM wants to know how this change affected the opportunity map. An enterprise research leader wants a stable, governed model or a clear version history—not an attractive framework whose dimensions vary across artifacts.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would show both models, the clustering or validation results, the reason each dimension survived or was removed, and a concrete activity that moved clusters after the change. It would also identify the accepted blind spot. If the four-axis system demonstrably improved interpretability and decision usefulness, the simplification shows judgment. If the mismatch is only an undocumented portfolio inconsistency, it weakens confidence in the architecture’s provenance.

## Answer

Both models are real, the six-scale version came first as the design model, and the four-axis version is what survived contact with the scoring instrument. The undocumented middle is a legitimate provenance complaint, so let me document it now.

The original conception — the one the portfolio image shows — had six scales: regulatory exposure, timing dependency, process modularity, value timeframe, knowledge transferability, market dispersion, with functional modality as the label describing what the activity is. That's the model I sketched when the industry-breakdown approach hit its stereotype problem: marketing inside pharma has less in common with pharma R&D than with marketing inside a late-night show, so compare conditions of work, not sectors. All six scales genuinely existed in the pipeline — when I rebuilt the visualization columns in April, I listed them verbatim: "regulatory exposure, timing dependency, market dispersion, process modularity, value time frame, and knowledge transferability."

The reduction happened at the tagging prompt, for instrument-reliability reasons rather than conceptual ones. The production prompt — a strict-CSV Industry Structure Evaluation Analyst persona, twenty-four modules per pass, no markdown, no commentary — scored functional modality plus four axes. Knowledge transferability and market dispersion were cut from the scored set because they failed in pilot passes in two different ways. Knowledge transferability asks how tacit the know-how is, and secondhand literature almost never says; the model scored it anyway, confidently, which made it precisely the kind of plausible-sounding column I'd learned not to trust. Market dispersion had the opposite failure: it collapsed toward "Global" for nearly everything, because the papers over-sample multinationals — a column with no variance discriminates nothing. So the scored instrument kept the four axes that had both textual evidence and spread: Regulatory Exposure, Timing Dependency, Process Modularity, Value Timeframe. Keeping all six would have preserved conceptual completeness at the cost of two low-trust columns. I optimized for the downstream use — these scores were about to become the clustering substrate — and low-trust columns poison clusters silently.

The stakeholder layer your question flags ran in parallel rather than driving the cut. The rapid conversations with the Head of AI and the Product Manager were about where on the axes to look — their sentiment, which I've quoted elsewhere, was toward boring, non-glamorous, hard-to-define areas AI hadn't reached. That steer shaped which axis combinations I read out (the 24 pairwise combinations of axis values, then the four clusters — high-regulation/short-term, low-regulation/long-term, coordinated-timing/modular, independent-timing/integral — that parented the archetypes). It did not decide which axes existed; that was the pilot-pass evidence.

What moved, concretely, when the model changed: activities differentiated mainly by knowledge tacitness lost their separation. Craft-heavy work — the luxury-goods material where apprenticeship and brand intuition carry the value — could no longer be distinguished axially from process-heavy work with similar regulation and timing profiles; it re-entered the synthesis through module content instead, which is partly why craftsmanship survives in the final matrix only as a theme-level tension (Craftsmanship vs. Brand Perception) rather than a structural dimension. That's the accepted blind spot, named.

None of this was written down at the time as a version decision. The portfolio narrative kept the six-scale conceptual framing because it's the truer picture of how I think about situations; the deliverable's triangulation shows four axes because that's what the data could support. Between them sits an inconsistency that a careful reader catches and the artifacts don't explain — you caught it. If I were governing this as a maintained framework, the fix is trivial: a model version history with a one-paragraph rationale per dropped dimension. I didn't keep one, and the reconstruction you've just read is exactly that paragraph, written a year late.
