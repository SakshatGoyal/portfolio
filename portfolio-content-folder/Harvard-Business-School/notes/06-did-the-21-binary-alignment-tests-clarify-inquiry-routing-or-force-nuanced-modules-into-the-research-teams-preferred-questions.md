# 6. Did the 21 binary alignment tests clarify inquiry routing, or force nuanced modules into the research team’s preferred questions?

**Category:** The work is understandable but potentially unconvincing  
**Caliber markers:** Decisions, craft, defensibility

**Question:** How were the 21 binary tests derived from the three research questions, how were thresholds and ties handled, and what evidence showed that assigning each module to its “strongest analytical lane” preserved rather than erased cross-cutting meaning?

**Reason for investigation:** Decomposing three broad questions into explicit tests is good systems design. It makes the routing logic inspectable and prevents a model from vaguely claiming relevance. The example, however, produces a set of checks and crosses that ends in one conclusion: the module supports “challenges faced in decision-making.” Strategic material often answers several questions at once. Binary alignment can create a clean matrix while hiding uncertainty, partial relevance, or dependence between questions.

This matters because the downstream themes and product opportunities inherit the routing assumptions. A public-product PM would want to know whether a different routing method changed prioritization. An enterprise AI leader would inspect calibration, reviewer agreement, confidence, and how the system handled modules that were relevant to both executive behavior and AI intervention.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want the exact tests, scoring rule, validation procedure, ambiguous cases, and sensitivity analysis. A strong answer would show a module whose initial route was overturned and what that changed downstream. If the binary structure was chosen primarily to make large-scale synthesis manageable, that may still be sound judgment—but the information loss and safeguards need to be explicit. Without that evidence, the architecture risks mistaking legibility for validity.

## Answer

The 21-question system was the recovery from a failed instrument, and the failure teaches more than the success. My first routing attempt was a 17-criterion weighted rubric — categories multiplied by importance weights I set by fiat ("Human-Centered & Strategic Depth, multiplier of 3... Stress Test Quality, multiplier of 1"). It failed in the most instructive way possible: score collapse. On the first batch run, all thirty modules received the identical total of 153. The rubric wasn't measuring; it was performing. Prompt hardening — module resets, duplicate-score audits, penalty rules — got concentration down from 30/30 to 17/30, still uselessly flat. Within the same day I abandoned weighted scoring entirely and rebuilt routing as a binary card sort, which is where your twenty-one questions come from.

The construction: each of the three research questions was broken into sub-questions — ten to thirty depending on the question — then filtered to seven distinct sub-questions each. Twenty-one total, seven per category: how executives make decisions, challenges in decision-making, AI's role. Each module scored 0 or 1 per question — does this module meaningfully help answer it — with worked anchors defining what earns a 1 (a Category-1 Q1 point requires an actual decision-under-uncertainty moment with rationale, not motivational "decisiveness" talk). Category totals out of seven; the max total wins the routing. The mechanics you'd probe: ties produced a combined tag ("Category 1 + Category 3"), explicitly treated as a valid hybrid rather than ambiguity to resolve; all-zeros meant INVALID and ejection; structural weirdness got flagged and scored anyway rather than skipped. Operationally it ran in fifteen-module batches — a model-context constraint, not a design choice — with a second prompt compiling thirty-module summary tables, values pulled only from prior outputs to stop the model inventing totals in the aggregate step.

Now your actual question: legibility versus validity. Binary scoring destroys information by design — a module that's 60% relevant to two questions scores identically to one that's 90% and 10%. I chose that loss consciously, for a practical reason: the previous instrument proved that this model class, at this task, could not sustain calibrated graded judgment across hundreds of items — it collapsed to a safe template. Binary relevance with worked anchors was the strongest claim the system could make repeatably. The tie-tag mechanism was the pressure valve that kept genuinely cross-cutting modules from being flattened; a meaningful minority of modules carried combined tags, which is the system admitting the gray your team note describes.

I ran no sensitivity analysis comparing routing methods, measured reviewer-agreement study, or confidence scoring. I cannot show you a module whose route was overturned by human review and changed a downstream theme, because routing review was spot-checking, not protocol. What I can show is the opposite kind of evidence — a whole routing ambition that got killed. The RQ1/RQ2 interpretive layers, which tried to route by latent meaning rather than relevance, were retired on validity grounds: secondhand literature can't support first-order latent-meaning claims. The 21-question layer survived precisely because it asked less — "can this module help answer this question" is a claim about usefulness, not hidden truth. So did the tests force modules into preferred questions? Somewhat, inevitably: the seven sub-questions per category encode what I thought each research question meant, and downstream themes inherit that. The safeguard wasn't statistical; it was that routing only determined which analytical lane read a module first — the synthesis passes that built archetypes went back to module text and source papers, so a mis-routed module was mis-shelved, not silenced. That's a real mitigation and a partial one, and I'd rather you know its exact size than believe a cleaner story.
