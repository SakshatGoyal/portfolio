# 15. How were interpretive depth and model quality judged without turning the designer’s preferred answer into the gold standard?

**Category:** The work is understandable but does not yet go far enough  
**Caliber markers:** Decisions, provenance, defensibility

**Question:** Who scored responses as literal, contextual, well-reasoned, intuitive, or imaginative; what rubric and reference answers did they use; were reviewers blind to model identity and prompt version; and how did you measure agreement, cost, latency, and failure severity alongside repeatability?

**Reason for investigation:** The designer correctly recognized that consistent output can remain shallow and that creative output can be unstable. The “degrees of interpretive depth” model and the eight blind iterations attempt to evaluate both. Yet the categories are themselves interpretive, and the folder does not show reviewer count, inter-rater reliability, blinded samples, a human or domain-expert benchmark, or how “intuitive” was separated from plausible invention. Later prompt refinements may also have encoded preferred examples, improving apparent agreement by narrowing the model toward the designer’s framing.

This is not only a technical concern. The chosen model shaped 992 modules, clusters, archetypes, and product recommendations. A public-product PM wants to know whether better scoring predicted more useful decisions. An enterprise AI leader needs an evaluation system that survives new models, new reviewers, and adversarial cases.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would provide the rubric, blinded protocol, reviewer roles, agreement statistics, failure taxonomy, held-out set, and examples where a domain expert rejected a model’s elegant interpretation. It would also explain routing by risk and the cost-quality tradeoff. If the evaluation was primarily the designer comparing outputs by judgment, it is valuable exploratory practice but should not be presented as a robust model benchmark.

## Answer

They were not fully protected from that. I was the only rater. Every "literal" through "imaginative" judgment was mine. There was no second reviewer, no inter-rater statistic, no domain-expert benchmark panel, and any account of this evaluation that implies otherwise is inflated. What existed were guardrails that made my judgment inspectable and harder for me to fool — which is a different, weaker, real thing.

Guardrail one: judgment was externalized into reference documents before scoring, not improvised during it. For each tagging system I wrote down what ideal tagging looked like, worked examples, and — critically — tiebreaker rules for the ambiguous cases, before running evaluations. The five degrees of interpretive depth were anchored by definition and example: literal restates the text; contextual situates it; well-reasoned builds a supported chain; intuitive reads meaning the text implies but doesn't state — the layer strategic research actually needs; imaginative departs from the source, which sounds like a compliment and functioned as a defect flag, because unmoored invention was the failure mode I most feared. Committing definitions in advance doesn't eliminate bias, but it converts taste into criteria that another person could audit and apply — and those documents survive.

Guardrail two: the blind protocol, described at its true size. Blind meant model-blind, not rater-blind: outputs from parallel runs were compared without model labels attached during the read, so my known fondness for GPT-4.5's readings couldn't consciously drive the depth call. It did not mean I was blind to prompt version — I authored the versions — and with weeks of exposure, model voices become recognizable, so de-labeling attenuates rather than removes identification. Eight iterations, same source data, evolving prompts and references; consistency measured mechanically as two-run overlap (the mechanical half of the protocol, since overlap doesn't care about my preferences), depth judged by me against the anchors.

Guardrail three: distrust of my own summary statistics. The Module 37 incident is the emblem: one categorical module scored twice in fresh windows returned 153 and 152 — near-identical totals — while the criterion-level reasoning underneath had moved materially, bias-visibility up, penalties applied differently. A stable total concealing unstable judgment. After that, evaluation stopped trusting final scores and inspected criterion-level decisions, justification quality, and recurring score patterns — which is also how the 153-concentration pathology was caught and prompt-hardened from 30/30 to 17/30.

Now the specific trap you named, because it's the sharpest: iterative prompt refinement can encode the designer's preferred answer, "improving" agreement by narrowing the model toward my framing. I can't fully acquit myself; adding examples is exactly how such encoding happens. Three things pushed back. The devil's-advocate stage (Prompt 1B) existed to attack extractions, structurally rewarding disagreement with the first pass. The iteration-eight regression — consistency falling to ~72% after a refinement I believed in — showed the system retained the power to disappoint me, which pure preference-encoding wouldn't. And the depth scale penalized the flattering failure: sycophantic elaboration scored imaginative, not intuitive. Cost and latency were tracked only informally — batch sizes and throughput judgments, o1-pro's 500-module proposition weighed qualitatively; no failure-severity taxonomy was ever built, and "does it align with what a human would do" meant, operationally, this human.

The resulting boundary is clear: this was a practitioner's evaluation harness — pre-committed rubrics, adversarial stages, mechanical repeatability checks, criterion-level auditing — run by a single rater to decide which tools to trust for one pipeline. It is defensible as exploratory practice. As a model benchmark it should convince no one, because n-of-one interpretation is its foundation, and the main lesson I carried out is that the eval's biggest unmeasured variable was me.
