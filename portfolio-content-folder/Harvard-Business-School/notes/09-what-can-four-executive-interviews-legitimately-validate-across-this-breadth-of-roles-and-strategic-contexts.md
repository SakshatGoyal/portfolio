# 9. What can four executive interviews legitimately validate across this breadth of roles and strategic contexts?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Provenance, defensibility, consequence

**Question:** How were the four participants recruited, which research questions did they address, what did their roles and industries leave uncovered, and which claims were changed, rejected, or deliberately left speculative after the interviews?

**Reason for investigation:** The interview section is transparent about the participants: a VP of commercial strategy, a director of digital products, a pharmaceutical strategy and product leader, and a chief compliance officer. Their quotations produce useful tensions around frameworks, informal networks, sensitive data, human judgment, analytics, and coordination. But four people cannot validate a system spanning many industries, executive levels, sixteen scenarios, four archetypes, and seven design-principle pairs. The artifacts do not show the discussion guide, duration, analysis method, saturation, disconfirming evidence, or mapping from each participant to the larger framework.

A public-product PM would ask whether the sample represents an initial discovery signal or a basis for product scope. An enterprise research leader would examine recruitment bias, seniority differences, confidentiality constraints, and whether literature-derived hypotheses shaped the interviews too strongly.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** A strong answer would define the intended evidentiary role of the interviews, provide the protocol and coding approach, show contradictions, and identify claims the team downgraded because evidence was thin. I also want the next-sample plan tied to the recommended MVP. If the interviews were correctly used to challenge—not validate—the literature system, the modest sample can still be valuable. If they are presented as broad confirmation, the story overreaches.

## Answer

Almost nothing, because they were never intended to. Four people cannot validate a system spanning six strategy classifications, four archetypes, sixteen scenarios, and seven principle pairs, and any telling of this project that uses the word "validated" near these interviews is overreaching. Their legitimate role was asymmetric: interviews can falsify, complicate, and ground a literature-derived structure; they cannot confirm it. One executive contradicting the framework is signal; four executives failing to contradict it is not validation, it's a small sample behaving politely.

The recruitment was pragmatic, not sampled: four senior leaders reachable through the institute's and our networks — Akhil Shah (VP Commercial Strategy & Corporate Development, Cityblock Health), Timothy Miller (Director of Product, Squarespace), Karen Levine (RSV marketing, strategy and product management, Sanofi), Dennis Irwin (Chief Compliance Officer, Alkami Technology). Health tech, product tech, pharma, and regulated fintech; VP-to-C-suite. What the sample leaves uncovered, said plainly: no manufacturing, energy, retail, or public-sector voice despite those sectors' weight in the corpus; no CEO; no non-US perspective; skewed toward the digitally fluent — the exact bias my sourcing rule existed to counter, reappearing in the primary research where I had less control over access. A recruitment I designed against my bias produced a corpus; a recruitment constrained by reachability produced these four. I note the asymmetry rather than excuse it.

What the interviews were used for, concretely. First, to pressure-test RQ3 from inside real constraints, and here they earned their cost: Tim's formulation — fully unskeptical that a generative model could produce a list of blind spots, fully skeptical it could de-noise that list — is a sharper statement of the synthesis-versus-judgment boundary than anything in my 992 modules, and it moved the design principles toward the postures that keep the human as the de-noiser (Second Lens, Deliberative). Second, to surface the trust architecture: Karen's "my summer intern" enthusiasm coexisting with hard lines about what artifacts may touch external models; Dennis's "AI gives output; judgment is still required." Those four voices converged on the Technology-and-Human-Tension theme from the inside, which is the closest thing to corroboration the interviews provide — and I weight it as convergence, not proof, because the theme was already in the literature synthesis before they spoke.

Third — and this is the part that's uncomfortable — one deliverable artifact leans harder on them than four interviews can bear: the Craftsmanship vs. Brand Perception cell opens its evidence section with "research from executive interviews reveals," attributing a tension to a four-person sample. The tension is real in the modules too, but that sentence, in production, borrows more authority from the interviews than they hold, and as the person who wrote the provenance rules I mark it as a place my own artifact under-enforced them.

What was changed or downgraded after the interviews: the sensitive-data constraint moved from a peripheral consideration to a first-order adoption ceiling in the takeaways — the deliverable says outright that the assistant's ceiling is trust architecture, not capability. What was deliberately left speculative: everything in the sixteen cells the interviews didn't touch, which is most of them, and which is why eleven [speculated] flags sit across the sixteen cells' success criteria.

There was no formal coding of transcripts, no inter-rater analysis, no saturation claim — with n=4, claiming saturation would be self-parody. The next-sample plan was tied to the recommended scope: if the team took Coordinated Builders, recruit at the craftsmanship-brand intersection specifically — one cell even says so in its own text. The interviews were a reality check on a literature machine, correctly sized as one, except for the one sentence where they weren't.
