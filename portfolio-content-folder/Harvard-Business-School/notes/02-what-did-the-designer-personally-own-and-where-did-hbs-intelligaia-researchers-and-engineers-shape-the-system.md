# 2. What did the designer personally own, and where did HBS, Intelligaia, researchers, and engineers shape the system?

**Category:** Missing information or evidence  
**Caliber markers:** Provenance, force multiplication, corroboration

**Question:** Across the research brief, paper selection, prompt design, insight-module schema, model testing, classification logic, clustering, archetypes, interviews, scenario writing, design principles, scope recommendations, and final presentation, what was uniquely yours, what was jointly authored, and what was inherited or generated?

**Reason for investigation:** The portfolio narrative alternates between “I” and “we,” while the final deck is credited to Intelligaia and Harvard’s Digital Data Design Institute. The story says the designer created a novel mapping approach, wrote the principles, and kept the pipeline goal stable across iterations. The deliverables also contain extensive research, speculative writing, visual design, and AI-generated or AI-assisted material. Yet there is no precise responsibility map, team composition, decision-rights model, or collaborator account.

This matters especially in an AI-assisted research project because a polished artifact does not reveal who framed the prompts, validated sources, chose the taxonomy, edited outputs, designed the visuals, or approved the recommendations. A public-product PM wants to know who owned the product judgment. An enterprise leader needs to see how design, research, domain expertise, and engineering governed one another.

**Learning objective and decision relevance:** I want a clear “I/we/system” map: the designer’s decisions, collaborators’ decisions, model-generated material, human revisions, and sponsor approvals. A strong answer would name one disagreement that changed the architecture, one contribution the designer deliberately did not claim, and collaborators who could corroborate the work. Precise attribution would make the project’s technical and strategic ambition more credible. If the boundary remains broad, it becomes difficult to distinguish Staff-level leadership from skilled synthesis and presentation of a collective effort.

## Answer

I keep an explicit attribution ledger for this project because the work is AI-assisted end to end, and in AI-assisted work an unmapped "we" is indistinguishable from a cover story. The map has four registers: mine, the model's, the team's, and inherited.

Mine: the three research questions and the decision to keep them separated; the diagnosis of my own sampling bias and the contrast-maximization rule that countered it; the insight-as-juxtaposition definition; the rubric weights, set by fiat and defended as fiat; the consistency and interpretive-depth evaluation program; the decision to abandon the HDBSCAN route on April 10; the situational-axes concept; the anti-flattening corrections; the principle/counter-principle method; the scope recommendations; every final editorial call. The brief was one sentence; everything downstream of that sentence was my responsibility, including the mistakes.

The model's, disclosed as such: candidate industry lists (I supplied the veto and the "most contrasting five" rule, not the raw lists); the substance of the module schema's middle block — on March 18 the model proposed a format I explicitly rejected ("I don't necessarily like this format. However, I find all the aspects you're discussing here extremely critical") and I grafted its questions into my two-part structure, making it three parts; and the personas-to-archetypes reframe, which the model proposed during the week-15 crisis and I accepted because the data supported it. That last one matters: the single most quotable conceptual move in the project — "archetypes don't have to be personas; they could be decision-making orientations" — was not originally mine. I claim the judgment to accept it and the data discipline that justified it, not the sentence.

The team's: five Intelligaia colleagues each wrote candidate design principles from the same RQ3 evidence base; my contribution was the synthesis, the overlap analysis, and the plausibility audit of their counter-principles. The four interviews were conducted with named executives whose words I quote, not paraphrase. The engagement structure belonged to Intelligaia and D³.

The disagreement that changed the architecture: my own hypothesis going into the principles synthesis was that one senior colleague's set was categorically different from the others. The overlap analysis partly confirmed it — distinct in lens and compression rather than in kind — and two of that set's names ("Deliberative vs. Synthesis," on-demand frameworks) survived into the final seven precisely because the analysis, not my preference, said they were the distinct ones.

The contribution I deliberately do not claim is validation. The HBS-side Head of AI and Product Manager shaped scope continuously — the pivot toward "boring," non-glamorous, hard-to-define work areas came out of conversations with them, and I cannot reconstruct which of us first said it. When authorship of a framing is genuinely blurred, I mark it blurred.

This responsibility map is reconstructed from 700-plus retained chat exports and pipeline files, and it is only as good as what the archive captured. The archive captures my dialogues with the models nearly completely and my dialogues with humans barely at all — stakeholder conversations happened on calls that left no transcript. So my attribution of model contributions is evidence-backed to the timestamp, while my attribution of human contributions rests on memory and would need the Head of AI and the PM to corroborate. That asymmetry is itself a finding about AI-era provenance: the machine's role in my work is better documented than my colleagues'.
