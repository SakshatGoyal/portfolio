# Portfolio case study collaboration brief

## What this document is

A working surface — for thinking, and for a conversation with a collaborator — not yet a brief to execute against. It will eventually inform how the portfolio gets built in Fable, but right now its job is to sharpen the goal, not to instruct.

The end goal is a portfolio of six case studies. Two concerns sit underneath it: the content and storytelling, and the visual design. Both matter, and in the end they are inseparable — this is not text with images attached, but a choreographed sequence where text, images, video, and diagrams each carry part of the argument. For now, though, the harder and more urgent problem is content and storytelling, so that is where this document points.

## The reader we're serving

Broadly, anyone senior enough to be judging this work — product managers and senior PMs, senior and staff product designers, design leaders. If we have to name one reader to write toward, it is the senior-most hiring team at a mid-sized to large FAANG company, evaluating staff-level design work. Aiming at that reader holds the bar high without narrowing the work to a single role.

## The problem we actually have

There is no shortage of material. The opposite is true: there is too much, spread across project folders, old and new versions, images, videos, and separate projects that need to be fused into one. Two things are wrong with it as it stands. It is long. And — despite being real work — it reads as if it might not have been. Something in the volume and the framing makes serious work look thinner than it was.

That second problem is the one to keep in view. The fix is not more explanation. It is judgment made visible.

## The failure we are trying to avoid

The single largest risk is that an AI, asked to write a portfolio, reaches for what it has absorbed from fifty thousand other portfolio sites — the confident, rounded, interchangeable voice of portfolio-speak. That voice is the tell. It is what makes real work read as generic.

What we want instead is harder to earn: a genuine point of view, formed from what this specific work actually was, from what design leaders at this level actually look for, and from a wider base of knowledge — and then case studies that are *crafted*, not merely written. That distinction is the whole exercise.

## Roadmap

Three phases, each with a clean handoff. A phase does not begin until the one before it has produced its output, so early bias cannot leak downstream.

### Phase 1 — Define the standard. No contact with the material.

Detailed in [phase-1-brief.md](phase-1-brief.md). In short:

The objective is not "criteria for a good portfolio" — that produces the generic answer we're avoiding. It is to **define the design judgment and experience a senior, staff, or lead designer must demonstrably have to be credible to a design leader in 2026**, as a portable standard that governs the portfolio *and* the resume, profile, and interview alike. Grounded in what leaders actually reward — reconnaissance shows polish is now free, the portfolio demoted from evidence to ante, and visible, defensible judgment the thing being screened for — not in portfolio convention, and written before any contact with our own material so it can't be bent to flatter it.

Output: a written standard, portable across assets.

Gate: specific enough to *fail* real work, survives the swap test (design caliber, not "any senior knowledge worker"), and assumes polish is free rather than rewarding it.

### Phase 2 — Make sense of the material.

Objective: understand what is actually in each of the six projects before any design begins.

Fable goes folder by folder — the current site and script.js, panw-uploads, docusign-bob-uploads, onereport-uploads, hbs, Cisco-Ready, Hitachi-Energy, and the asset and Figma exports — reading every version, image, video, and note, in the role of the person who did the work. For each case it surfaces the people problem, the major decisions and pivots, the outcome as metric *and* behavior change, the strongest available evidence, and what is missing.

Output: a per-case synthesis — one honest account of what each case is really about and what it can prove.

Gate: each synthesis names one hard decision the case can make visible. If it cannot, that is flagged, not smoothed.

### Phase 3 — Design, critique, iterate.

Objective: six case studies, designed in web, each carrying equal weight.

For each case, Fable produces the case study as web design — story, image composition, captions — then runs three loops: build or revise → critically evaluate against the Phase 1 criteria → iterate. The critique is adversarial, not confirmatory: it hunts for where the case reads generic, where a metric fails to land, where tension got smoothed, where a scanning eye would miss the point. Contrasting directions are welcome early and are measured against the criteria, not taste.

Output: six iterated web case studies, and for each a short record of what every loop changed and why.

Gate: after the third loop, each case is scored against the criteria; anything that does not clear the bar is named as such, not shipped quietly.

## Six cases, equal weight

Six is a lot to hold at this level, and the honest reduction — cutting to three or four — is off the table here: we don't control which project a reader opens, or in what order. So the rule is equal weight. Whatever stays in the portfolio has to meet the same standard on its own, because any one of them might be the only one someone reads.

The first case is already written. The intent is for Fable to break the material down on its own and write the remaining cases — reworking the first where exploration warrants it — rather than have the decomposition handed to it. The per-case notes below are source context and current instinct, not a finished breakdown.

## How people actually read

A quieter failure sits beneath the generic-voice problem, and it may matter more. AI writes as though being written is the same as being read — as though a true sentence on a page has done its job. It hasn't. People read with their eyes, not by absorbing tokens. They scan, they skip, they don't finish sentences, and they don't read everything inside an image either. A metric buried mid-paragraph is, for most readers, invisible.

So the standard is not "is it written and accurate" but "will it survive the way a real person actually moves across the page." That is a perception problem before it is a writing problem, and it governs everything downstream: what gets pulled out, what gets compressed, what earns a caption or a number of its own, and what stays in the body only because it must. This is the real weight behind *write less, carry more.*

## Working constraint

Write less, carry more. The main body of each case stays restrained — roughly 480 words where possible — while captions, images, video, and diagrams do more of the explanatory work. The writing should not flatten ambiguity into a tidy story, but it also should not make the reader dig through process debris to find the point.

The current site is source material, not the verdict. The newer directions, old case studies, rough diagrams, screenshots, prototypes, notes, and outside project folders all count as evidence. We go case by case, gather the material first, then decide what the case needs to prove.

## The writing model

The current Palo Alto case is the closest reference we have for how a case should read — not because it's finished (it isn't), but because of how it divides labor between words and images. Ekin Tumer's Multi-view case does the same thing more strictly. Read as a guide, not a template, the pattern is:

The body text carries only three loads: the *people problem* being solved, the major design *decisions, challenges, and pivots*, and the *outcome*. Everything procedural — research, workshops, wireframes, prototype architecture — is pushed out to images and terse captions. The words say what mattered and why; the pictures show how it was done.

Compression, but with the tension left in. The Palo Alto case says users were "indifferent," that verification cost "outweighed the benefits," that it was "uncomfortable being yet another team trying to add a chatbot." None of that is smoothed into a clean arc. The friction is the evidence that the work was real.

The turns are reframes, stated plainly. "The challenge wasn't transparency, but the effort spent on verification." "The question was never 'how do we add a chatbot' but 'what assumptions do we carry that will fail our users.'" A major design decision reads as a single sentence that changes what the reader thought the problem was.

Load-bearing phrases are surfaced for the scanning eye — a bold clause, a named move ("Anchored Follow-Ups," "Trace"), a short labeled section. This is the perception principle made concrete: the one phrase that must survive a scan is the one made catchable.

Outcome is a metric *and* a behavior change, paired. The metric says something happened; the behavior change says it mattered. Engagement "over twice industry standard" is the number; "it gave the team the push to speed up data engineering" and "users asked to snapshot the response into Slack and Salesforce" are the behavior changes that make the number mean something. By the author's own read, the behavior changes are the part not yet written with enough force — and the part a reader remembers.

The caution stands: this is how these two cases happen to work, not a mold to pour every case into. A different project may need a different shape. What transfers is the division of labor and the honesty, not the sequence of sections.

## Source map

Current site text and case structure live mainly in script.js. Current visual exports live in assets/figma-nodes, assets/figma-elements, and assets/project-tiles. Palo Alto source material is in panw-uploads. DocuSign Data Analytics material is in docusign-bob-uploads. OneReport material is in onereport-uploads. HBS material is in hbs. Reference portfolio captures and prior cover-page thinking live in portfolio-site-studies. The newly added Cisco-Ready and Hitachi-Energy folders should be treated as source folders for the next two cases.

Two setup problems are known and unsolved. First, the material is scattered across these locations and versions, so before Fable can work a case we either consolidate its sources into one place or point Fable precisely at them. Second, impact metrics exist as rough written notes per project; they need to be added and then written so they *land* — surfaced where a scanning eye will catch them, not dropped in as a lone sentence. There is no NDA constraint on what can be shown.

## 1. Palo Alto Networks

Current direction: narrow the case around AI interaction rather than the full Sales Workbench story. The newer Figma direction centers anchored follow-ups, traceability, verification cost, and impact. The case should still preserve enough context to explain why AI was being introduced into a sales operations platform, but the main story is no longer platform consolidation.

Known materials include the current Palo Alto case, panw-uploads, existing Figma exports, motion/prototype material, and the new Figma design direction. Missing or unresolved: final high-resolution images, the exact missing impact metric, and which parts of the older consolidation story should remain as context rather than become a parallel case.

## 2. Harvard Business School

Current direction: AI research architecture for strategic thinking. The useful story is not simply that AI supported research, but that the work had to structure an abstract field before useful personas, principles, or strategic use cases could be discussed.

Known materials include the current HBS case, hbs source files, research pipeline visuals, triangulation maps, situational axes, and human-AI principle framing. Missing or unresolved: the sharpest way to explain the research architecture without turning the case into a methods report.

## 3. DocuSign Data Analytics

Current direction: internal analytics moving from visibility toward action. The case should cover Global Data Analytics and Book of Business as decision-tool work, not as a generic dashboard portfolio piece.

Known materials include the current GDA case, docusign-bob-uploads, past GDA images, feature usage material, and Book of Business artifacts. Missing or unresolved: which story leads the case, whether Feature Usage and Book of Business remain one case or become two chapters inside one case, and what outcome evidence is strongest enough to foreground.

## 4. DocuSign OneReport

Current direction: mobile analytics for executive use. The case should make clear that this was not a smaller dashboard, but a separate executive-use context: checking data before the day starts, finding a number during a meeting, or deciding what deserves deeper analysis later.

Known materials include the current OneReport case, onereport-uploads, scenario images, Reports, Analysis, and AMA explorations. Missing or unresolved: how much executive context is needed before showing the product surfaces, and whether the AMA/search decision should be framed as an AI-cost decision or an interaction-design decision.

## 5. Hitachi Energy

Current direction: to be defined after reviewing the newly added Hitachi-Energy folder. Treat the old case study as raw source material, not as the final shape of the story.

First task: inventory the old case, images, diagrams, and any available notes. Then identify the main decision or transformation that makes the case worth including alongside the other five.

## 6. Cisco

Current direction: combine two Cisco Ready markdown stories into one case study. The goal is not to paste two stories together, but to find the shared spine between them.

First task: read both markdown files in Cisco-Ready, identify what each one is really about, then decide what the combined case can prove that neither story proves alone.

## Still open

Two problems are named on purpose and left unsolved for now. What exactly makes real work read as unreal — the instinct is real even when its trigger can't yet be named; adding impact metrics is a partial hedge, not the answer, and we return to it. And how to calibrate the writing to the author's own voice rather than a generic one — the editor question, now anchored by the Palo Alto and Ekin cases as voice references but not yet resolved. The perception problem above is different: not a thing we finish, but a standing constraint we hold throughout.

## Operating standard

A case earns its place when one hard decision becomes visible through the right evidence. If a reader cannot see the decision, the case is either the wrong case or the wrong cut of it.
