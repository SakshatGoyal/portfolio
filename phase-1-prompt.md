# Phase 1 — the brief, as I'd explain it to you

Here's what I'm trying to do, and why I'm handing you this particular piece first, on its own.

I'm building a design portfolio — six case studies — but the portfolio sits downstream of a problem I haven't actually solved, and I don't want to start building until it is. The problem is figuring out what makes a senior, staff, or lead designer read as *credible* in 2026 — credible to the people who decide whether to hire them. I want you to work that out and write it down before either of us goes near a single one of my cases.

The sequencing is deliberate, so let me be clear about it. If we define the bar after looking at my work, we'll bend the bar — unconsciously, but we will — to flatter what I already happen to have. So this first piece happens blind. Don't go digging through my project folders or the existing site yet; that's a later phase. Right now I only want the standard, defined on its own terms.

One reframe up front, because it changes what you're actually looking for. The instinct is to ask "what makes a good portfolio." That's the wrong question — it pulls a generic, portfolio-shaped answer, and generic is the exact thing I'm trying to escape. The real question is about the *person*: what does someone at this level have to demonstrably have — in experience, in ability, in whatever the real markers turn out to be — to be taken seriously? Answer that, and the answer travels — it governs the portfolio, but also the resume, the LinkedIn profile, how a case gets talked through out loud in an interview. Define the caliber once; everything else gets measured against it.

The reader I'm writing all of this toward, if we have to name one: the senior-most hiring team at a mid-to-large FAANG-scale company, evaluating staff-level design work. Aiming there keeps the bar high without narrowing it to a single job title.

Here's the concern underneath all of this — the thing I most want the standard to account for. Some of my own work reads as though it might not have been real, even though it was. That feeling is newer than the work is; something has changed recently in what a portfolio can and can't prove, and I don't think the old signals mean what they used to. I have my own theory about why, and I ran an earlier pass of research of my own — but I'm deliberately keeping both out of this brief. If I hand you my conclusion, you'll confirm it back to me, and what I actually need is your independent read.

So treat a real part of this as investigative: work out what has actually shifted in what a portfolio proves, and what design leaders now screen for as a result. Let that shape the standard — but it should be a conclusion you reached by looking, not an echo of mine. How you go about the research is your call; I care about where you land, not the route.

One steer on where to look, since it's the part I'd most want done well. The most honest material lives in the most anonymous places — Reddit design and UX communities, Blind, and the like — where leaders and senior designers say the unguarded things they'd never put on a stage or a LinkedIn post. The candor there is the whole point. So go after that raw register directly: get into what people actually vent, admit, and argue about when their name isn't attached. If a source is genuinely unreachable, say so plainly rather than papering over it — I'd rather know where the evidence is thin than have a gap quietly smoothed.

So, concretely, what I want back from you: a written standard that names — specifically, and in a way that can actually be tested against real work — what a person has to make visible to read as senior, staff, or lead caliber to a design leader in 2026. Grounded in what those leaders actually reward, not in portfolio convention, and written so it holds up across any asset, not just the portfolio.

I'll know it's good if it can *fail* real work — if I can hold an actual case study against it and it tells me "this doesn't show it," rather than just describing something nice in the abstract. If it survives a swap test: it should describe *design* caliber specifically, not something you could say about any competent senior knowledge worker. And if it reckons honestly with how things have changed recently, rather than restating what a portfolio was supposed to prove five years ago. Get that right and the next phases inherit a real rubric instead of a mood.
